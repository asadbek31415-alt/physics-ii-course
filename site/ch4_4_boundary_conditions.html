Deep Dive: Boundary Conditions & Shielding

When magnetic fields pass from one material to another (e.g., from air to iron), the fields change abruptly. To solve problems involving interfaces, we derive Boundary Conditions from the integral forms of Maxwell's Equations.

1. The Rules of the Interface

1. Normal Component of B

From the divergence law \(\nabla \cdot \bfB = 0\), applying the Divergence Theorem to a small "pillbox" spanning the interface tells us that the magnetic flux entering must equal the flux leaving.

$$ B_{1}^{\perp} = B_{2}^{\perp} $$

The perpendicular component of \(\bfB\) is continuous.

2. Tangential Component of H

From Ampere's Law for H: \(\oint \bfH \cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{f,enc}\). Applying this to a small loop straddling the interface, assuming no free surface current (\(\bfK_f = 0\)):

$$ H_{1}^{\parallel} = H_{2}^{\parallel} $$

The parallel component of \(\bfH\) is continuous.

These seemingly simple rules have profound consequences. Since \(\bfB = \mu \bfH\), the condition \(H_{1}^{\parallel} = H_{2}^{\parallel}\) implies: $$ \frac{B_{1}^{\parallel}}{\mu_1} = \frac{B_{2}^{\parallel}}{\mu_2} \implies B_{2}^{\parallel} = \frac{\mu_2}{\mu_1} B_{1}^{\parallel} $$ If Material 2 is highly permeable iron (\(\mu_2 \gg \mu_1\)), the parallel magnetic field inside the iron is huge! This "sucks" magnetic field lines into the material.

2. Example: Magnetic Shielding

🛡️ The Problem

Suppose we have a hollow spherical shell of high-permeability iron (\(\mu\)) placed in a uniform external magnetic field \(\bfB_0\). What is the field inside the empty cavity?

Step 1: The Potential Method

Since there are no free currents, we can use the magnetic scalar potential \(W\) (where \(\bfH = -\nabla W\)) satisfying Laplace's equation \(\nabla^2 W = 0\).

Step 2: Apply Boundary Conditions

We match \(W\) at the inner radius \(a\) and outer radius \(b\) using the continuity rules derived above.

Step 3: The Result

After significant algebra, the field inside the cavity \(\bfB_{in}\) is found to be uniform and parallel to the external field, but reduced by a factor:

$$ \bfB_{in} = \frac{9\mu_r}{(2\mu_r + 1)(\mu_r + 2) - 2(a/b)^3(\mu_r - 1)^2} \bfB_0 $$

For high permeability (\(\mu_r \gg 1\)), this simplifies to: $$ \bfB_{in} \approx \frac{9}{2\mu_r (1 - (a/b)^3)} \bfB_0 $$

Because \(\mu_r\) is in the denominator and can be very large (e.g., 1000 for soft iron), the field inside is a tiny fraction of the external field. The iron shell captures the flux lines and routes them around the cavity, effectively shielding the interior.
