Deep Dive: The Divergence of E

We have seen the integral form of Gauss's Law, which applies to large, macroscopic regions. But what if we want to describe the electric field at a single point in space? For this, we need the concept of Divergence.

1. What is Divergence?

Mathematically, divergence is an operator that takes a vector field and outputs a scalar function. Physically, it measures how much a vector field spreads out (diverges) from a specific point.

The "Faucet" Analogy

Imagine the vector field represents the flow of water.

  • Positive Divergence: The point is a source (like a faucet turned on). Water is being created there and flowing outwards.
  • Negative Divergence: The point is a sink (like a drain). Water is flowing inwards and disappearing there.
  • Zero Divergence: The water is just flowing through. Whatever flows in, flows out. No water is created or destroyed.

In electrostatics, electric charge acts as the "source" or "sink" of the electric field. Positive charge creates outward flow (positive divergence), and negative charge creates inward flow (negative divergence).

Visualizing sources (Positive Divergence) and sinks (Negative Divergence).

2. Deriving the Differential Form

Where does the equation \(\nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \rho/\epsilon_0\) come from? It comes directly from the integral form of Gauss's Law combined with the Divergence Theorem.

Step 1: Start with the Integral Form

$$ \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0} $$

Step 2: Rewrite Charge as an Integral

The enclosed charge \(Q_{\text{enc}}\) is simply the integral of the volume charge density \(\rho\) over the volume \(\mathcal{V}\):

$$ Q_{\text{enc}} = \int_{\mathcal{V}} \rho \, d\tau $$

Step 3: Apply the Divergence Theorem

The Divergence Theorem is a purely mathematical tool that relates the flow through a surface to the divergence inside the volume:

$$ \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = \int_{\mathcal{V}} (\nabla \cdot \vec{E}) \, d\tau $$

Step 4: Comparison

Now substitute both integrals back into Gauss's Law:

$$ \int_{\mathcal{V}} (\nabla \cdot \vec{E}) \, d\tau = \int_{\mathcal{V}} \left( \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} \right) \, d\tau $$

Since this must hold true for any volume \(\mathcal{V}\) we choose, the terms inside the integrals (the integrands) must be equal at every point. Thus, we arrive at Maxwell's First Equation:

$$ \nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} $$

3. How to Calculate Divergence

To solve problems, you need to calculate \(\nabla \cdot \vec{E}\) using the appropriate coordinate system.

Cartesian Coordinates (x, y, z)

  • $$ \nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z} $$

Cylindrical Coordinates (s, φ, z)

  • $$ \nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{1}{s}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}(s E_s) + \frac{1}{s}\frac{\partial E_\phi}{\partial \phi} + \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial z} $$

Spherical Coordinates (r, θ, φ)

  • $$ \nabla \cdot \vec{E} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(r^2 E_r) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial \theta}(\sin\theta E_\theta) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial E_\phi}{\partial \phi} $$
