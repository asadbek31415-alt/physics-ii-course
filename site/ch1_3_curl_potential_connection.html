Deep Dive: Derivation & The Curl Condition

The formula for electric potential is not arbitrary. It is derived directly from the physical concepts of Work and Energy, subject to the mathematical condition that the field is conservative.

1. The Physical Origin: Work and Energy

Imagine moving a test charge \(Q\) from point \(a\) to point \(b\). The electric force exerted on the charge is \(\vec{F} = Q\vec{E}\). The Work done by this force is:

$$ W_{a \to b} = \int_a^b \vec{F} \cdot d\vec{l} = Q \int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} $$

Because the electrostatic force is conservative, the work done is equal to the negative change in Potential Energy (\(U\)). Think of lifting a rock against gravity: you do work, and the rock gains potential energy.

$$ W_{a \to b} = - \Delta U = -(U_b - U_a) $$

The Distinction between \(U\) and \(V\):
Potential Energy (\(U\)) depends on the size of the test charge \(Q\). To get a property of the field itself, we define Electric Potential (\(V\)) as the potential energy per unit charge:

$$ V \equiv \frac{U}{Q} $$

Combining these equations, we get the fundamental relation for potential difference:

$$ V_b - V_a = - \int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} $$

2. The Reference Point \(\mathcal{O}\)

The equation above only calculates the difference in potential. To define \(V(\vec{r})\) as a specific number, we must pick a reference point \(\mathcal{O}\) where we agree that \(V=0\).

$$ V(\vec{r}) = - \int_{\mathcal{O}}^{\vec{r}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} $$

Changing the Reference Point

There is an essential ambiguity here. If we choose a different reference point \(\mathcal{O}'\), we are essentially adding a constant \(K\) to the potential everywhere.

$$ V'(\vec{r}) = V(\vec{r}) + K $$

Does this matter? No.

  • Potential Difference: \((V'_b - V'_a) = (V_b + K) - (V_a + K) = V_b - V_a\). The constant cancels out.
  • Electric Field: Since \(\vec{E} = -\nabla V\), and the derivative of a constant is zero, \(\nabla (V + K) = \nabla V\). The physical field remains the same.

3. Mathematical Necessity: Path Independence

For the definition of potential to make sense, the integral \(\int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}\) must depend only on the start and end points, not the path taken.

Let's check this for a point charge \(q\). Integrating in spherical coordinates, the dot product \(\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}\) picks out only the radial component:

$$ \int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_{r_a}^{r_b} \frac{1}{r^2} dr = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left[ \frac{1}{r_a} - \frac{1}{r_b} \right] $$

The result depends only on \(r_a\) and \(r_b\). The path does not matter.

4. The Curl Condition

If the integral is path independent, then integrating around a closed loop must yield zero. By Stokes' Theorem, this implies the Curl is zero everywhere.

$$ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0 \iff \nabla \times \vec{E} = 0 $$
⚠️ Impossible Electrostatic Fields

This condition is strict. If you encounter a vector field \(\vec{F}\) where \(\nabla \times \vec{F} \neq 0\), then \(\vec{F}\) cannot be an electrostatic field. A scalar potential \(V\) cannot exist for such a field.

5. Visualizing Curl-Free Fields

A field with zero curl is "irrotational." It has no intrinsic swirl. This is what allows us to define a unique potential height \(V\) at every point.
