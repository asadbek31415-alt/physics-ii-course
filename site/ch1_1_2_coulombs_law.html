Deep Dive: Coulomb's Law

The Fundamental Law of Force

In 1785, Charles Coulomb published the results of a series of delicate experiments using a torsion balance. He discovered that the force between two stationary point charges is:

$$ \vec{F} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{\rsep^2} \hatrsep $$

This equation contains several critical pieces of information:

  • Magnitude: The force is proportional to the product of the charges ($q_1 q_2$) and inversely proportional to the square of the separation distance ($\rsep^2$).
  • Direction ($\hatrsep$): The force acts along the line joining the two charges. If $q_1 q_2 > 0$ (like charges), the force is repulsive. If $q_1 q_2 < 0$ (opposite charges), it is attractive.
  • Vector meaning: The force is a vector. The scalar factor $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_1q_2}{\rsep^2}$ sets the magnitude scale, with its sign encoding attraction or repulsion, while the unit vector supplies the direction from the source charge position $\vec{r}'$ to the test charge position $\vec{r}$.
  • Point Charges: Strictly speaking, this law applies only to point charges. For extended objects, we must use integration (superposition).

🧪 Experiments to Try

  1. Inverse Square Law: Place the charges at a specific distance (e.g., 4 grid squares). Note the force. Move them to half that distance (2 squares). Does the force quadruple?
  2. Linearity of Charge: Keep the distance constant. Double the value of $q_1$. Does the force vector exactly double in length?
  3. Zero Force: Set one of the charges to 0 μC. The force vanishes, confirming that interaction requires two parties.

Why the Weird Constant? ($1/4\pi\epsilon_0$)

You might wonder why we write the constant as $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$ instead of just a simple $k$.

The factor $4\pi$ is purely geometric. It comes from the surface area of a sphere ($4\pi r^2$). Because the electric influence spreads out spherically from a point source, this $4\pi$ appears naturally in the denominator.

The constant $\epsilon_0$ is called the permittivity of free space. $$ \epsilon_0 \approx 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \, \frac{\text{C}^2}{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2} $$ It is essentially a scaling factor that makes the units work out. By defining the constant this way, we make later equations (like Gauss's Law) much simpler and more elegant, removing the $4\pi$ factor from them.

Analogy with Gravity

Coulomb's Law looks suspiciously similar to Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation. Both are inverse-square laws, suggesting a deep geometric connection (flux spreading out in 3D space).

Feature Electrostatics Gravity
Formula $F = k \frac{q_1 q_2}{\rsep^2}$ $F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$
Source Electric Charge ($q$) Mass ($m$)
Sign Positive or Negative Always Positive
Interaction Attracts or Repels Always Attracts
Strength Extremely Strong ($k \approx 9 \times 10^9$) Very Weak ($G \approx 6.67 \times 10^{-11}$)

The key difference is strength. The electric force between an electron and a proton is about $10^{39}$ times stronger than the gravitational force between them! This is why we can usually ignore gravity when studying atomic physics.
