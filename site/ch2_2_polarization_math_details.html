Deep Dive: The Definition of \(\vec{P}\)

From discrete molecules to a continuous vector field.

In a physical dielectric, the dipole moment is not spread out smoothly like butter; it exists as discrete "lumps" at specific atomic locations. However, dealing with \(10^{23}\) discrete vectors is impossible. We need a smooth approximation.

The Discrete Definition

Consider a small volume \(\Delta V\). It contains \(N\) atoms, each with a dipole moment \(\vec{p}_i\). The total dipole moment in this volume is the vector sum:

$$ \vec{P} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec{p}_i}{\Delta V} $$

The Continuous Limit

To do calculus, we take the limit as the volume shrinks. But there is a catch! If we shrink \(\Delta V\) too much (smaller than an atom), the volume will mostly contain empty space, and the value of \(\vec{P}\) will fluctuate wildly between "huge" (inside an electron cloud) and "zero" (between atoms).

Therefore, when we write the integral definition:

$$ \vec{P}(\vec{r}) = \frac{d\vec{p}}{d\tau} $$

We implicitly mean a "Macroscopic Infinitesimal". The volume \(d\tau\) is small enough to be considered a point for calculus, but large enough to contain thousands of atoms so the statistical average is smooth.

Wait, Coulombs per square meter (\(C/m^2\))? Those are the units of Surface Charge Density (\(\sigma\)).

This is not a coincidence! As we will see in the next section, the Polarization \(\vec{P}\) physically manifests itself as bound charge smeared over the surface of the material.
