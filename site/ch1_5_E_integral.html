Deep Dive: Calculating Fields: The Integral Method

We have established that for continuous distributions, the electric field is the integral of all infinitesimal charge elements $dq$. Combining this with continuous charge densities, the Electric Field at a position $\vec{r}$ generated by a continuous volume of charge is:

$$ \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_V \frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{\rsep^2} \hatrsep \, d\tau' $$

Note on vectors in integrals: The unit vector $\hatrsep$ depends on the position of the source element $dq$. As you integrate over the volume, $\hatrsep$ changes direction. Therefore, it cannot be treated as a constant and must remain inside the integral.

While this formula is general, applying it requires setting up a coordinate system calculation. Let's look at three classic geometries.

1. Infinite Line Charge (Rod)

Consider an infinitely long straight wire with uniform linear charge density $\lambda$. We want to find the field at a distance $z$ from the wire.

Field lines radiate outward from the rod. Notice how the density of arrows decreases as you move away.

The Calculation

We employ direct vector notation. Let the infinite line charge lie along the $x$-axis ($\lambda = \text{const}$) and calculate the field at a point $P = z\hat{z}$ on the $z$-axis.

1. Geometry & Vectors:

$$ \vec{r} = z\hat{z}, \quad \vec{r}' = x\hat{x}, \quad dl' = dx $$ $$ \vecrsep = z\hat{z} - x\hat{x} $$ $$ \rsep = \sqrt{z^2 + x^2} $$

2. The Integral (Infinite limits $-\infty \to \infty$):

$$ \vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\lambda (z\hat{z} - x\hat{x})}{(z^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} dx $$

3. Evaluation:

(Calculation skipped on purpose.)

4. Result:

$$ \vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2\lambda}{z} \hat{z} $$

Conclusion: For a line charge, the field falls off as $1/z$ (or $1/r$). This is slower than a point charge ($1/r^2$) because the line extends infinitely in one dimension.

2. Infinite Plane

Now consider an infinite flat sheet with uniform surface charge density $\sigma$.

Field vectors are uniform. Notice that the arrow length does not change regardless of distance from the plate.

The Calculation Setup

  1. Strategy: Divide the plane into concentric rings of radius $r$ and width $dr$.
  2. Charge Element: The area of a ring is $dA = 2\pi r \, dr$. The charge is $dq = \sigma (2\pi r \, dr)$.
  3. Field Element ($dE$): By symmetry, only the vertical component $dE_z$ survives. The distance is $\sqrt{r^2+z^2}$. $$ dE_z = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{dq}{r^2 + z^2} \cos\theta = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{\sigma 2\pi r \, dr}{r^2 + z^2} \frac{z}{\sqrt{r^2 + z^2}} $$
  4. The Integral Setup: $$ E = \frac{\sigma z}{2\epsilon_0} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{r}{(r^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} dr $$

    (Calculation skipped on purpose.)

Result:

$$ E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} \hat{n} $$

Conclusion: The field is constant. It does not depend on the distance from the plane! This is a surprising result of the "infinite" assumption: as you move further away, you "see" more of the plane, which compensates exactly for the $1/r^2$ drop-off.

3. Solid Sphere

Finally, consider a solid sphere of radius $R$ with uniform volume charge density $\rho$.

Field increases linearly inside the sphere, then falls off like a point charge outside.

The Integral Setup

To find the field at a point $P$ on the $z$-axis (distance $z$ from center), using the full vector integral form:

  • Volume Element: $d\tau = r'^2 \sin\theta' dr' d\theta' d\phi'$. Charge density $\rho$.
  • Separation Vector: $\vecrsep = z\hat{z} - \vec{r}'$. Magnitude $\rsep = \sqrt{z^2 + r'^2 - 2zr'\cos\theta'}$.
  • The Integral: $$ \vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int \frac{\rho \hatrsep}{\rsep^2} d\tau = \frac{\rho}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_{0}^{R} \int_{0}^{\pi} \int_{0}^{2\pi} \frac{z\hat{z} - r'\hat{r}'}{(r'^2 + z^2 - 2r'z \cos\theta')^{3/2}} r'^2 \sin\theta' dr' d\theta' d\phi' $$

    (Calculation skipped on purpose.)

Result:

Outside ($z > R$): $$ \vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{z^2} \hat{z} $$ Inside ($z < R$): $$ \vec{E}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q z}{R^3} \hat{z} $$

Conclusion: Outside: The sphere acts exactly like a point charge $Q$ at the origin.
Inside: The field increases linearly with distance from the center. At the center ($z=0$), the field is zero.

🚀 A Better Way: Gauss's Law

Did you find these integrations hard? Don't worry, there is a better way.

In the next chapter, we will introduce Gauss's Law. It allows us to solve these exact problems (Line, Plane, Sphere) in usually one or two lines of algebra, by exploiting the symmetry of the situation.

However, Gauss's Law only works easily for symmetric cases. The integral method you learned here is the universal tool—it works for any shape, no matter how ugly, provided you can do the math (or ask a computer to do it).
