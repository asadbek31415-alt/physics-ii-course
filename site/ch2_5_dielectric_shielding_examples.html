Deep Dive: Dielectric Shielding Examples

Here we explore two classic boundary value problems that demonstrate how dielectrics modify electric fields. In both cases, the dielectric material reduces ("shields") the electric field, but the geometry dictates exactly how.

1. Dielectric Sphere in a Uniform Field

Problem: A sphere of radius \(R\) made of linear dielectric material (\(\epsilon\)) is placed in an otherwise uniform electric field \(\vec{E}_0\). Find the field inside the sphere.

The Solution Strategy

Since there is no free charge (\(\rho_f = 0\)), the potential satisfies Laplace's Equation \(\nabla^2 V = 0\). We solve this using separation of variables in spherical coordinates, applying boundary conditions at \(r=R\).

Boundary Condition 1 (Potential): \( V_{in} = V_{out} \)
(Potential is continuous)
Boundary Condition 2 (Displacement): \( \epsilon \frac{\partial V_{in}}{\partial r} = \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial V_{out}}{\partial r} \)
(Normal component of D is continuous because \(\sigma_f = 0\))

The Result

After solving for the coefficients (Legendre polynomials), we find a remarkable result: the field inside the sphere is uniform.

$$ \vec{E}_{in} = \frac{3}{\epsilon_r + 2} \vec{E}_0 $$

Since \(\epsilon_r > 1\), the field inside is always weaker than the external field. The induced bound charges on the surface create an internal field that opposes \(\vec{E}_0\).

2. Metal Sphere with Dielectric Shell

Problem: A metal sphere of radius \(a\) carries a charge \(Q\). It is surrounded by a linear dielectric shell of permittivity \(\epsilon\) out to radius \(b\). Find the potential at the center relative to infinity.

Step 1: Find Displacement (D)

This problem has spherical symmetry and free charge \(Q\) at the center. Gauss's Law for \(\vec{D}\) is the fastest tool:

$$ \vec{D} = \frac{Q}{4\pi r^2} \hat{r} \quad \text{(everywhere outside metal)} $$

Step 2: Find Electric Field (E)

We convert \(\vec{D}\) to \(\vec{E}\) using the local permittivity:

Region Permittivity Electric Field (\(\vec{E}\))
Inside Shell (\(a < r < b\)) \(\epsilon\) \( \frac{Q}{4\pi \epsilon r^2} \hat{r} \)
Outside Shell (\(r > b\)) \(\epsilon_0\) \( \frac{Q}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r^2} \hat{r} \)

Step 3: Calculate Potential (V)

We integrate from infinity to the surface of the metal (\(r=a\)): $$ V = - \int_{\infty}^{b} \vec{E}_{out} \cdot d\vec{l} - \int_{b}^{a} \vec{E}_{in} \cdot d\vec{l} $$

$$ V(a) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{b} + \frac{1}{\epsilon_r} \left( \frac{1}{a} - \frac{1}{b} \right) \right) $$

Notice that if \(\epsilon_r = 1\) (vacuum), this reduces to the standard Coulomb potential \(Q/4\pi\epsilon_0 a\). With the dielectric, the potential is reduced because the dielectric "shields" the charge.
