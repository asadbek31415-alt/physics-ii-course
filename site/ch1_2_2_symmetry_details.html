Deep Dive: Applying Gauss's Law

Gauss's Law is always true, but it is not always useful. To use it to calculate the electric field, we need to be clever. We need to find a surface where the integral becomes trivial.

The "Cheat Code" of Electrostatics

Usually, we integrate to find the flux. Here, we do the reverse: we use symmetry arguments to determine the shape of the field, which allows us to pull \(E\) out of the integral. Then, we use algebra to solve for \(E\).

The Algorithm

For any symmetric distribution (Sphere, Cylinder, Plane), the process is identical:

1. Geometry

Argue that \(E\) is constant and \(\perp\) to surface.

\(\oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = E \cdot A\)
=

2. Physics

Count the charge inside.

\(\frac{Q_{\enc}}{\epsilon_0}\)
⇒

3. Solve

Isolate E.

\(E = \frac{Q_{\enc}}{\epsilon_0 A}\)

1. Spherical Symmetry

Consider a spherical shell or solid sphere of charge. If you were to rotate the sphere, the charge distribution would look identical. Therefore, the resulting Electric Field must also look identical after rotation.

This forces the field to be Radial (pointing straight out) and its magnitude to depend only on the distance \(r\) from the center.

The Derivation

We draw a Gaussian Sphere of radius \(r\).

  • LHS (Flux): Since \(\vec{E}\) is parallel to \(d\vec{a}\) everywhere, \(\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = E da\). Since \(E\) is constant at radius \(r\), we pull it out: \[ \oint E da = E \oint da = E (4\pi r^2) \]
  • RHS (Charge): \(Q_{\enc} / \epsilon_0\)
  • Result: \[ E (4\pi r^2) = \frac{Q_{\enc}}{\epsilon_0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q_{\enc}}{r^2} \hat{r} \]

Conclusion: Outside the sphere, it acts exactly like a point charge. Inside a hollow shell, \(Q_{\enc}=0\), so \(E=0\).

2. Cylindrical Symmetry

Consider an infinite line of charge or a long pipe. The symmetry dictates that the field must point radially away from the axis (the \(s\) direction). It cannot point along the wire (\(z\)) or around it (\(\phi\)), or a flip/rotation would contradict the setup.

The Derivation

We draw a Gaussian Cylinder of radius \(s\) and length \(L\).

  • LHS (Flux): Flux through the top and bottom caps is zero (field is parallel to surface). Flux only goes through the curved side (Area \(2\pi s L\)). \[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = E (2\pi s L) \]
  • RHS (Charge): The charge inside is the linear density \(\lambda\) times the length \(L\). \[ Q_{\enc} = \lambda L \]
  • Result: \[ E (2\pi s L) = \frac{\lambda L}{\epsilon_0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 s} \hat{s} \]

3. Planar Symmetry

Consider an infinite flat sheet of charge. If you move parallel to the sheet, nothing changes. Therefore, \(E\) cannot depend on \(x\) or \(y\). It must point perpendicular to the plane.

The Derivation

We draw a "Gaussian Pillbox" that extends through the sheet.

  • LHS (Flux): The field points straight up and straight down. No flux passes through the curved sides. Flux only passes through the top and bottom lids (Area \(A\) each). \[ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = EA_{\text{top}} + EA_{\text{bottom}} = 2EA \]
  • RHS (Charge): The pillbox cuts out a patch of area \(A\) on the sheet. \[ Q_{\enc} = \sigma A \]
  • Result: \[ 2EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\epsilon_0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{E} = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} \hat{n} \]

Conclusion: Remarkably, the field of an infinite plane is constant. It does not get weaker as you move further away.
