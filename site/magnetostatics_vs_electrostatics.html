Deep Dive: Charge Conservation, Steady Current, and KCL

The continuity equation is the general mathematical statement of charge conservation. The condition $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}=0$ follows only when the charge distribution is steady. The same conservation law will later become Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) when we describe a junction as a circuit node.

Step 1: Global Conservation of Charge

Consider a volume $V$ enclosed by a surface $S$. The total charge $Q$ inside this volume is the integral of the charge density $\rho$:

$$ Q(t) = \int_V \rho(\mathbf{r}, t) \, d\tau $$

Since charge is conserved, the only way the total charge inside $V$ can decrease is if charge flows out through the surface $S$. Therefore, the current flowing out ($I_{out}$) must equal the rate of decrease of the enclosed charge:

$$ I_{out} = \oint_S \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{a} = - \frac{dq}{dt} $$
Step 2: Applying the Divergence Theorem

We can rewrite the total current $I_{out}$ using the Divergence Theorem to convert the surface integral into a volume integral:

$$ \oint_S \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{a} = \int_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}) \, d\tau $$

Simultaneously, we move the time derivative inside the charge integral (assuming the volume $V$ is fixed in space):

$$ - \frac{dq}{dt} = - \frac{d}{dt} \int_V \rho \, d\tau = - \int_V \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \, d\tau $$
Step 3: The Continuity Equation

Equating the two volume integrals from Step 2:

$$ \int_V (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}) \, d\tau = - \int_V \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \, d\tau $$

Since this must hold true for any arbitrary volume $V$, the integrands themselves must be equal. This yields the Continuity Equation, which is the general law and applies whether or not the current is steady:

$$ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0 $$

Translation: Current flows out of a point ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}$) exactly as fast as the charge density there decreases ($-\partial \rho / \partial t$).

Step 4: The Steady Current Condition

A steady current is defined as a flow where the charge distribution does not change with time. While charge is moving, it is not piling up anywhere.

$$ \text{Steady State} \implies \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} = 0 $$

Substituting this into the continuity equation:

$$ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = - (0) \implies \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = 0 $$

From a Volume to a Circuit Node

Now surround a circuit junction with a small closed surface. The integral continuity equation says that any imbalance between the currents entering and leaving that node must appear as stored charge:

$$ \sum I_{\mathrm{in}}-\sum I_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{dQ_{\mathrm{node}}}{dt} $$

In the lumped, steady-state circuit limit, the node does not accumulate appreciable charge. Setting $dQ_{\mathrm{node}}/dt\approx0$ gives Kirchhoff's current law:

$$ \sum I_{\mathrm{in}}=\sum I_{\mathrm{out}} $$

A charging capacitor plate shows why the general equation matters. Conduction current reaches the plate, but charge cannot cross the dielectric, so the plate charge changes and $dQ/dt\neq0$. This does not violate charge conservation; it is precisely the term that a careless steady-state KCL argument would omit. In a circuit model we define the capacitor current by $I_C=dQ/dt$, so the stored charge is kept in the bookkeeping rather than ignored.
