Deep Dive: Properties of Electrostatic Energy

1. Where is the Energy Stored?

We have two mathematically equivalent formulas for the same energy \(W\):

$$ W = \frac{1}{2} \int \rho V d\tau \quad \text{vs} \quad W = \int u_e d\tau = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} \int E^2 d\tau $$

The first formula suggests that the energy is stored in the charge (where $\rho$ is non-zero). The second suggests it resides in the field, with an energy density ($u_e$):

$$ u_e = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} E^2 $$

In the context of electrostatics, these are simply two ways of looking at the same thing. However, in electrodynamics (when fields can move and carry momentum), we are forced to conclude that the field viewpoint is the physically correct one. Energy is a real thing distributed through space.

2. Superposition Failure

The principle of superposition states that \(\vec{E}_{tot} = \vec{E}_1 + \vec{E}_2\). However, this does not apply to energy. Because the energy depends on the square of the field, the total energy is not simply the sum of individual energies.

$$ E^2 = (\vec{E}_1 + \vec{E}_2)^2 = E_1^2 + E_2^2 + 2\vec{E}_1 \cdot \vec{E}_2 $$

Integrating this gives:

$$ W_{tot} = W_1 + W_2 + \epsilon_0 \int \vec{E}_1 \cdot \vec{E}_2 d\tau $$

The last term is the interaction energy. It accounts for the work done to bring the two systems together.

3. The Self-Energy Problem

Paradox: The integral \(\int E^2 d\tau\) is always positive. Yet, we know that bringing a positive and negative charge together releases energy (negative work). How can the field formula give a negative value?

It doesn't. The field formula \(W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} \int E^2 d\tau\) calculates the total energy, including the energy required to create the point charges themselves (their self-energy).

For a true "point" charge, the self-energy is infinite, because the field goes to infinity as \(1/r^2\) at the origin, and its square (\(1/r^4\)) integrated over a volume creates a divergent integral.

In discrete particle physics, we usually ignore this infinity and only calculate the interaction energy (the sum formula). In continuous distributions, the self-energy is finite and is automatically included in the integral.
