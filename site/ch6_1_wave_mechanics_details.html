Deep Dive: Mathematics of Waves

The 1-D Wave Equation

A wave is any disturbance \(f(x,t)\) that preserves its shape as it moves. The most general form of such a wave is governed by the 1D partial differential equation:

$$ \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = \frac{1}{v^2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial t^2} $$

The General Solution: d'Alembert proved that any function of the form \(f(x,t) = g(x - vt) + h(x + vt)\) satisfies this equation. This means any shape—square pulses, pulses shaped like Gaussian bells, or complex signals—can be a wave if its time-evolution is coupled to its spatial position in this specific way.

Extending to 3D

In three dimensions, the disturbance may depend on \(\vec{r} = (x, y, z)\). The scalar wave equation then uses the Laplacian operator (\(\nabla^2\)):

$$ \nabla^2 f = \frac{1}{v^2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial t^2} $$

Where \(\nabla^2 f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2}\). Solutions to this equation include Plane Waves (spanning all space in two directions) and Spherical Waves (which decay as \(1/r\) from a point source).

Sinusoidal Waves

While the wave equation allows for any function \(f(x,t) = g(x-vt)\), the most practical and mathematically significant solutions are Sinusoidal Waves. They serve as the fundamental building blocks for all other waves via Fourier Analysis.

$$ f(x, t) = A \cos(kx - \omega t + \delta) = \text{Re}\{ \tilde{A} e^{i(kx - \omega t)} \} $$

Here, \(\tilde{A} = Ae^{i\delta}\) is the complex amplitude, which simplifies calculations by packing both the physical amplitude \(A\) and the phase shift \(\delta\) into a single complex number.

The Exponential Advantage: Using complex notation, spatial and temporal derivatives become simple algebraic multiplications: $$ \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \to -i\omega, \quad \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \to ik $$ This transforms partial differential equations into linear algebraic ones. We only take the Real Part at the final step of any physical calculation.
$\lambda$ Wavelength. The distance over which the wave repeats ($2\pi/k$).
$T$ Period. The time for one full oscillation ($2\pi/\omega$).
$v$ Phase Velocity. The speed at which a wave crest moves ($v = \lambda/T = \omega/k$).

The Principle of Superposition

Since the wave equation is linear, if $f_1$ and $f_2$ are solutions, then any linear combination $a f_1 + b f_2$ is also a solution. This allows us to build complex wave packets from simple sinusoidal waves using Fourier Analysis.
