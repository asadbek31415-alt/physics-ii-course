Polarization

Electric response of matter

Matter is not passive. When you place a piece of matter in an electric field, the material responds. This response is called Polarization. We distinguish between two main mechanisms:

  • Induced Dipoles: Neutral atoms stretching (electron cloud shifts relative to nucleus).
  • Alignment of Polar Molecules: Pre-existing dipoles rotating to line up with the field.

4.1.3 Alignment of Polar Molecules

Some molecules, like water, have permanent dipole moments built into their structure due to electronegativity differences. In the absence of an external field, these dipoles point in random directions.

When exposed to a uniform electric field \(\vec{E}\), the field exerts a torque on the dipole:

$$ \vec{N} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E} $$

This torque tries to line the dipole up parallel to the field. If there were no other forces, every single molecule would align perfectly.

Thermal Agitation vs. Alignment

In reality, we never see perfect alignment. Why? Because the molecules are constantly jostling around due to thermal energy. The universe is a battleground between two competing forces:

  1. Electric Force: Tries to order the system (minimize potential energy).
  2. Thermal Collisions: Tries to disorder the system (maximize entropy).

The Energy Ratio

The degree of alignment depends crucially on the ratio of the potential energy gained by alignment to the thermal energy available to disrupt it.

$$ \text{Ratio} \approx \frac{pE}{k_B T} $$

Where \(p\) is the dipole moment, \(E\) is the field strength, \(k_B\) is the Boltzmann constant, and \(T\) is temperature.

The potential energy of a dipole in a field is \( U = -\vec{p} \cdot \vec{E} = -pE \cos\theta \). The probability of finding a dipole at angle \(\theta\) follows the Boltzmann distribution \(\exp(-U/k_B T)\).

For standard temperatures and fields, the thermal energy \(k_B T\) is usually much larger than the electric dipole energy scale \(pE\). This means the alignment is actually quite weak—only a small fraction of molecules effectively line up.

This statistical average leads to the Langevin Formula. For moderate fields (\(pE \ll k_B T\)), the average dipole moment points along \(\vec{E}\) and is proportional to \(1/T\):

$$ \langle \vec{p} \rangle \approx \frac{p^2}{3 k_B T}\vec{E} $$

This explains Curie's Law (typically applied to magnetism, but the math is identical for electric dipoles): Polarization decreases as things get hotter.
