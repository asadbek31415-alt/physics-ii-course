The Continuity Equation

The Continuity Equation

The principle of local conservation of charge is a fundamental cornerstone of electrodynamics. Global conservation is not enough; charge cannot simply disappear from New York and instantly reappear in London. If the total charge within a specific region decreases, it must be because charge has physically flowed across the boundary of that region.

Let us quantify this. Consider an arbitrary volume $V$ bounded by a closed surface $S$. The total charge $Q(t)$ contained within this volume at any given time is the integral of the charge density $\rho$:

\[ Q(t) = \int_{\mathcal{V}} \rho(\mathbf{r}, t) \, d\tau \]

Now, consider the current flowing out of this region. The current density $\mathbf{J}$ represents the flow of charge per unit area, per unit time. Therefore, the total current $I$ passing outward through the surface $S$ is the flux of $\mathbf{J}$:

\[ I = \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{a} \]

Here lies the physical constraint: the current flowing out must equal the rate at which the charge inside is decreasing. We express this conservation law mathematically as:

\[ I = -\frac{dQ}{dt} \]

The negative sign is crucial; if positive current flows out, the internal charge $Q$ must be dropping. Substituting our integral definitions into this expression, we obtain:

\[ \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{a} = - \frac{d}{dt} \int_{\mathcal{V}} \rho \, d\tau \]

Assuming the volume $V$ is fixed in space (it does not expand or contract with time), we can move the time derivative inside the integral. Since $\rho$ depends on both position and time, the total derivative becomes a partial derivative:

\[ \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \mathbf{J} \cdot d\mathbf{a} = - \int_{\mathcal{V}} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \, d\tau \]

We now have a surface integral on the left and a volume integral on the right. To compare them directly, we invoke the Divergence Theorem (Gauss's Theorem) to convert the surface integral into a volume integral:

\[ \int_{\mathcal{V}} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}) \, d\tau = - \int_{\mathcal{V}} \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \, d\tau \]

Grouping the terms under a single integral sign, we find:

\[ \int_{\mathcal{V}} \left( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \right) d\tau = 0 \]

Now, recall that the volume $V$ we chose was entirely arbitrary. We could have chosen a sphere, a cube, or a microscopic bubble anywhere in space. The only way the integral can vanish for any choice of volume is if the integrand itself is zero everywhere. Thus, we arrive at the continuity equation:

\[ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{J} = -\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \]

This is the precise mathematical statement of local charge conservation: the divergence of the current density is equal to the rate of depletion of charge density at that point.
