Deep Dive: Basic Conductor Properties

On the main page, the conductor properties were stated compactly. Here, we slow down the logic: free charges move until the internal field is zero, and the other properties follow from that one equilibrium condition.

1. Why the Interior Field Must Vanish

A conductor contains charges that are free to move. If the total electric field inside the conducting material were not zero, those charges would feel $\vec{F}=q\vec{E}$ and begin to flow.

When an external field is applied, the mobile charges do move at first. They pile up on different parts of the surface, producing an induced electric field of their own. That induced field points opposite to the original field inside the metal. The redistribution continues until the two fields cancel there.

$$ \vec{E}_{\text{total}}=\vec{E}_{\text{external}}+\vec{E}_{\text{induced}}=0 \quad \text{inside the conductor} $$

This final state is what "electrostatics" means here. The conductor is not merely called electrostatic; it has reached a state where any remaining internal field would restart charge motion, so equilibrium demands $\vec{E}=0$ throughout the conducting material.

2. What Follows from $\vec{E}=0$

Once the field inside the conductor is zero, Gauss's law immediately fixes the volume charge density:

$$ \nabla \cdot \vec{E}=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}, \qquad \vec{E}=0 \implies \rho=0 $$

This does not say that the conductor contains no charge anywhere. It says there is no net charge density in the interior. If the conductor carries excess charge, that excess must reside on the surface.

The same zero-field condition makes the conductor an equipotential:

$$ V(b)-V(a)=-\int_a^b \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{l}=0 $$

Finally, just outside the conductor, the electric field cannot have a component tangent to the surface. A tangential component would push surface charge sideways, so the charges would not be in electrostatic equilibrium. The outside field must therefore be normal to the surface.

3. The Field Just Outside the Surface

A small Gaussian pillbox crossing the surface gives the magnitude of the normal field just outside the conductor.

$$ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0} $$

The bottom face lies inside the conductor, where $\vec{E}=0$. The side flux vanishes as the pillbox is made very thin. Only the outside face contributes:

$$ E A = \frac{\sigma A}{\epsilon_0} \implies \vec{E}_{\text{outside}} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\hat{n} $$

This result explains the jump in the electric field at a charged conductor surface. The separate mechanical question of the force on that surface charge is handled in the electrostatic-pressure deep dive.
