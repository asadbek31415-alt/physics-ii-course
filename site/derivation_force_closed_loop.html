Deep Dive: The Magnetic Dipole

In electrostatics, you learned that a "Dipole" consists of two opposite charges ($+q$ and $-q$) separated by a distance. In magnetostatics, we don't have magnetic monopoles (no isolated "North" charge). So, what is the fundamental unit of magnetism?

1. Defining the Magnetic Dipole

The Fundamental Unit: The simplest magnetic object is not a pole, but a current loop.

Any closed loop of current acts exactly like a tiny bar magnet. It has a "North" side (where field lines exit) and a "South" side (where field lines enter). Because it has these two poles, we call it a Magnetic Dipole.

The Definition ($\vec{m}$)

We define a vector $\vec{m}$, called the Magnetic Dipole Moment, to represent the strength and orientation of this loop.

$$ \vec{m} = I \vec{A} $$
  • $I$: The current flowing in the loop.
  • $\vec{A}$: The area vector. Its magnitude is the area of the loop, and its direction is normal to the surface.

Direction (Right-Hand Rule): Curl your fingers in the direction of the current $I$. Your thumb points in the direction of $\vec{m}$ (and the North pole).

3. Proof: Zero Net Force (Uniform Field)

You might expect a magnet to be pulled by a magnetic field. However, in a uniform field, a pure dipole experiences no net force.

The total force is the integral of the Lorentz force along the wire:

$$ \vec{F} = \oint I (d\vec{l} \times \vec{B}) $$

If $\vec{B}$ is constant (uniform), we can factor it out of the integral:

$$ \vec{F} = I \left( \oint d\vec{l} \right) \times \vec{B} $$

The term $\oint d\vec{l}$ is the vector sum of all displacement steps around a closed loop. Since you end where you started, this sum is exactly zero. Thus:

$$ \vec{F}_{\text{net}} = 0 \quad (\text{Uniform } \vec{B}) $$

This is why a compass needle (a dipole) rotates to point North but doesn't fly out of your hand toward the North Pole.

4. Torque and Alignment

Even though the net force is zero, the forces on opposite sides of the loop generally don't align. This creates a Torque ($\vec{\tau}$).

As seen in the simulation above, the forces try to twist the loop so that the Magnetic Moment $\vec{m}$ aligns with the B-field.

$$ \vec{\tau} = \vec{m} \times \vec{B} $$

This torque is the mechanism behind Paramagnetism. The external field exerts a torque on atomic dipoles, trying to line them up, while thermal energy tries to randomize them.

5. Non-Uniform Fields (The Attraction)

If the field is not uniform (like near the pole of another magnet), the cancellation in Section 3 fails. The side of the loop in the stronger field feels a larger force than the side in the weaker field.

$$ \vec{F} = \nabla (\vec{m} \cdot \vec{B}) $$

Physical Intuition: Dipoles are attracted to regions of stronger magnetic field (if aligned). This explains why a permanent magnet picks up paperclips. The magnet induces a dipole in the paperclip ($\vec{m}$), and the gradient of the magnet's field ($\nabla \vec{B}$) pulls that dipole in.
