Deep Dive: Continuous Charge

Our definition of the electric field starts with superposition for discrete point charges ($q_1, q_2, \dots$). For a macroscopic object, however, there may be so many charge carriers that tracking every charge value and every exact position is not practical. We replace that discrete list with a charge density spread over the object's shape.

1. The Strategy: Integration as Summation

From Discrete to Continuous

In the summation model, each term comes from one charge at its own position. In the continuous model, each tiny volume element contributes a small charge $dq = \rho\,d\tau$.

The simulator shows the same field point $P$ in two views. In summation mode, the blue arrow selects one charge contribution at a time. In integration mode, the charge carriers fade into a continuous object; the yellow element $dq$ scans through the shape, building the integral element by element.

The same object viewed as many separate charges, then as a continuous density $\rho$ over a known shape.

2. Charge Density

How much charge ($dq$) is inside a specific chunk? That depends on the geometry of the object and how concentrated the charge is. We define charge density as the amount of charge per unit of space.

Visualizing the differential elements for Line ($dl'$), Surface ($da'$), and Volume ($d\tau'$).

There are three standard geometries:

  • Line Charge ($\lambda$): Charge per unit length (Coulombs/meter).
    $$dq = \lambda dl'$$
  • Surface Charge ($\sigma$): Charge per unit area (Coulombs/meter$^2$).
    $$dq = \sigma da'$$
  • Volume Charge ($\rho$): Charge per unit volume (Coulombs/meter$^3$).
    $$dq = \rho d\tau'$$

3. Total Charge

If the charge density is uniform, calculating the total charge $Q$ is simple multiplication. However, if the density varies from source point to source point (i.e., $\rho(\vec{r}')$ is a function of source position), we must integrate.

$$ Q = \int_{\mathcal{V}} dq = \int_{\mathcal{V}} \rho(\vec{r}') \, d\tau' $$

⚠️ Understanding the Terms

  • $\rho(\vec{r}')$: This is the charge density function. It tells you how concentrated the charge is at any specific location $\vec{r}'$. For a uniform object, this is just a constant number.
  • $\mathcal{V}$: This represents the region of space occupied by the object. The "shape" of the integral (its limits) is determined by this region.
  • $d\tau'$: This is the differential volume element (a tiny chunk of space). Its formula depends on the coordinate system you choose to fit the shape: $$ d\tau' = dx' dy' dz' \quad \text{(Cartesian)} $$ $$ d\tau' = r'^2 \sin\theta' dr' d\theta' d\phi' \quad \text{(Spherical)} $$

Note: We use the Greek letter $\tau$ (tau) for volume because the symbol $V$ is strictly reserved for Electric Potential (Voltage).

💡 One Formula for All Shapes:
This general formula adapts to the dimensionality of the object:

  • If $\mathcal{V}$ is a Line: $\rho \to \lambda$ and $d\tau' \to dl'$
  • If $\mathcal{V}$ is a Surface: $\rho \to \sigma$ and $d\tau' \to da'$
  • If $\mathcal{V}$ is a Volume: $\rho \to \rho$ and $d\tau' \to d\tau'$
