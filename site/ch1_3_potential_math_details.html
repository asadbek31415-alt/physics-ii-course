Deep Dive: Mathematical Details

This section explores the rigorous derivation of the electric potential, why the curl of \(\vec{E}\) vanishes, and how boundary conditions are derived.

1. Why is the Curl Zero?

The definition of potential hinges on the fact that \(\nabla \times \vec{E} = 0\). Let's prove this for a single point charge \(q\) at the origin. The field is:

$$ \vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} \hat{r} $$

If we calculate the line integral \(\int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l}\) in spherical coordinates, where \(d\vec{l} = dr\hat{r} + r d\theta\hat{\theta} + r\sin\theta d\phi\hat{\phi}\), the dot product picks out only the radial component:

$$ \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} dr $$

Integrating this from point \(a\) to point \(b\):

$$ \int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_{r_a}^{r_b} \frac{1}{r^2} dr = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left[ \frac{1}{r_a} - \frac{1}{r_b} \right] $$

Crucial Result: The integral depends only on the distances \(r_a\) and \(r_b\), not on the path taken. If the path is a closed loop (where \(r_a = r_b\)), the integral is zero.

By Stokes' Theorem, if the line integral around any closed loop is zero, the curl must be zero everywhere:

$$ \oint \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = 0 \iff \nabla \times \vec{E} = 0 $$

2. Deriving the Integral for Localized Charge

We know the potential of a single point charge \(q\) (setting the reference at infinity) is:

$$ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r} $$

By the Principle of Superposition, the potential of a collection of charges is simply the sum of their individual potentials. For a continuous distribution, we chop the charge into infinitesimal chunks \(dq = \rho(\vec{r}') d\tau'\).

Integrating over the entire volume of the charge distribution:

$$ V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int \frac{dq}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_{\mathcal{V}} \frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|} d\tau' $$

Where:

  • \(\vec{r}\) is the observation point.
  • \(\vec{r}'\) is the location of the charge element.
  • \(|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|\) is the distance between them (often denoted as script-r, \(\rcurs\)).

3. Deriving Boundary Conditions

What happens to \(V\) at a surface with charge density \(\sigma\)?

Continuity of V

The potential difference between two points \(a\) (just below) and \(b\) (just above) is:

$$ V_b - V_a = - \int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} $$

As the path length shrinks to zero (\(a \to b\)), the integral vanishes (unless \(\vec{E}\) is infinite, which it is not for surface charges). Therefore:

$$ V_{\text{above}} = V_{\text{below}} $$

Discontinuity of the Gradient

While \(V\) is continuous, \(\vec{E}\) is not. From Gauss's Law applied to a thin pillbox straddling the surface, we know the normal component of the field jumps:

$$ E^{\perp}_{\text{above}} - E^{\perp}_{\text{below}} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} $$

Since \(\vec{E} = -\nabla V\), the normal component is the derivative with respect to the normal direction \(n\). Substituting this in:

$$ \frac{\partial V_{\text{above}}}{\partial n} - \frac{\partial V_{\text{below}}}{\partial n} = -\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} $$

This equation tells us that the "kink" in the potential graph is directly proportional to the surface charge density.
