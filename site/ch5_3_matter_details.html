Deep Dive: Maxwell's Equations in Matter

When dealing with macroscopic materials, it's convenient to rephrase Maxwell's equations in terms of "free" charges and currents, hiding the complicated bound charges and currents within the auxiliary fields $\vec{D}$ and $\vec{H}$.

The Auxiliary Fields

We define:

$$ \begin{aligned} \vec{P} &= \text{Polarization} \\ \vec{M} &= \text{Magnetization} \\ \vec{D} &= \epsilon_0 \vec{E} + \vec{P} \quad \text{(displacement field)} \\ \vec{H} &= \frac{1}{\mu_0} \vec{B} - \vec{M} \quad \text{(H-field)} \end{aligned} $$

Maxwell's Equations (Macroscopic)

In terms of free charge density $\rho_f$ and free current density $\vec{J}_f$:

$$ \begin{aligned} \nabla \cdot \vec{D} &= \rho_f \\ \nabla \cdot \vec{B} &= 0 \\ \nabla \times \vec{E} &= -\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t} \\ \nabla \times \vec{H} &= \vec{J}_f + \frac{\partial \vec{D}}{\partial t} \end{aligned} $$

These are the equations used in most engineering applications, where we control $\rho_f$ and $\vec{J}_f$ on conductors, and the material properties ($\epsilon, \mu$) take care of the rest.
