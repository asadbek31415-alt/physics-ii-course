Deep Dive: Applications of Ampere's Law

Ampere's Law allows us to calculate magnetic fields with ease, provided the current distribution possesses enough symmetry. The general strategy involves four steps:

  1. Identify the symmetry of the problem.
  2. Choose an Amperian Loop such that $\mathbf{B}$ is constant or zero along the path.
  3. Calculate the line integral $\oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l}$.
  4. Calculate the enclosed current $I_{\text{enc}}$ and solve for $B$.

1. The Infinite Straight Wire

Consider a long straight wire carrying a steady current $I$. We want to find the magnetic field at a distance $r$ from the wire.

Step 1: Symmetry & Loop Choice

Due to Cylindrical Symmetry, the magnetic field must have the same magnitude at any point equidistant from the wire. The field lines form circles around the wire. We choose a circular Amperian loop of radius $r$, centered on the wire.

Step 2: Evaluate the Integral

Along this circle, the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ is always parallel to the path element $d\mathbf{l}$, and its magnitude $B$ is constant.

$$ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint B \, dl = B \oint dl = B(2\pi r) $$
Step 3: Solve

The total current enclosed by the circle is simply $I$. Equating the two sides:

$$ B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I \implies \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} \hat{\phi} $$

2. The Ideal Solenoid

A solenoid is a long coil of wire. An "ideal" solenoid is infinitely long with tightly wound turns. We assume the field outside is zero and the field inside is uniform.

Step 1: Symmetry & Loop Choice

Due to Translational Symmetry along the axis, the field must be uniform inside. We choose a rectangular Amperian loop of length $L$ and width $w$. One side is inside the solenoid, and the opposite side is outside.

Step 2: Evaluate the Integral

We break the integral into four segments:

  • Side 1 (Inside): $\mathbf{B}$ is parallel to $d\mathbf{l}$. Contribution = $B L$.
  • Sides 2 & 4 (Perpendicular): $\mathbf{B}$ is perpendicular to $d\mathbf{l}$. Dot product is 0.
  • Side 3 (Outside): $\mathbf{B} \approx 0$. Contribution = 0.
$$ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = B L + 0 + 0 + 0 = B L $$
Step 3: Solve

Let $n$ be the number of turns per unit length. The loop of length $L$ encloses $nL$ turns. Each turn carries current $I$. Therefore, $I_{\text{enc}} = (nL)I$.

$$ B L = \mu_0 (n L I) \implies B = \mu_0 n I $$
Insight: Why is the field outside zero?

In the derivation above, we assumed the field outside was zero. We can prove this using Ampere's Law.

Imagine a second rectangular loop placed entirely outside the solenoid. Let the side closest to the solenoid be at distance $a$ from the axis, and the far side be at distance $b$.

Since this loop is outside the coils, it encloses no current ($I_{\text{enc}} = 0$). The integral becomes:

$$ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = [B(a) - B(b)]L = \mu_0(0) = 0 $$

This implies that $B(a) = B(b)$. In other words, the magnetic field outside is independent of the distance from the axis. However, we know physically that the field must vanish as we go infinitely far away ($B \to 0$ as $r \to \infty$). If the field is constant everywhere outside and zero at infinity, it must be zero everywhere outside.

3. The Toroid

A toroid is essentially a solenoid bent into a donut shape. It has $N$ total turns tightly wound around a core.

Step 1: Symmetry & Loop Choice

Due to Azimuthal Symmetry, the field magnitude is constant along any circle concentric with the center of the torus. We choose a circular Amperian loop of radius $r$ inside the coils.

Step 2: Evaluate the Integral

Similar to the straight wire, the field is parallel to the path.

$$ \oint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = B(2\pi r) $$
Step 3: Solve

The loop encloses the central hole. Every single turn of the wire passes through the center of our loop. Since there are $N$ total turns, the enclosed current is $NI$.

$$ B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 N I \implies B = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2\pi r} $$

Note: Unlike the solenoid, the field inside a toroid is not uniform; it is stronger near the inner wall (smaller $r$) and weaker near the outer wall.
