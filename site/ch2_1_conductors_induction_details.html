Deep Dive: Induction & Charge Distribution

How exactly does a conductor rearrange its charge to kill the electric field inside? And why do lightning rods have sharp points? Here we explore the mechanics of induced charge.

1. Charge in a Cavity

Consider a neutral conductor of arbitrary shape containing a cavity. Suppose we place a point charge $+q$ somewhere inside this cavity. Since the material is a conductor, the electric field in the bulk material (the "meat" of the conductor) must be zero.

"Nature abhors a field inside a conductor. The conductor will do whatever is necessary—moving charges to surfaces—to cancel it out."

The Inner Surface

Draw a Gaussian surface $\mathcal{S}$ completely inside the conductor, enclosing the cavity.

$$ \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0} $$

Since $\vec{E}=0$ everywhere on $\mathcal{S}$, the integral is zero. Therefore, $Q_{\text{enc}} = 0$.

The enclosed charge consists of the point charge $q$ plus the induced charge on the inner wall $q_{\text{induced}}$. $$ q + q_{\text{induced}} = 0 \implies q_{\text{induced}} = -q $$

Conclusion: A charge $-q$ is induced on the inner surface of the cavity.

The Outer Surface

If the conductor was originally neutral, charge is conserved. If $-q$ moved to the inner wall, a charge of $+q$ must be left over. Since no charge can exist in the bulk, this $+q$ must reside on the outer surface.

Crucial Asymmetry:
1. The distribution of $-q$ on the inner wall depends on where $+q$ is located in the hole.
2. The distribution of $+q$ on the outer surface does not depend on the location of $+q$. The outer charge distributes itself solely to make the field inside the conductor zero, which usually means spreading out as evenly as the outer geometry allows.

2. Surface Charge and Curvature

We stated that charge resides on the surface, but is it spread uniformly? Generally, no.

For a sphere, symmetry demands uniformity. For an irregular shape, charge accumulates at sharp points.

The "Two Spheres" Model

Imagine two metal spheres of radius $R_1$ and $R_2$ connected by a long thin wire. Because they are connected, they form a single conductor and must be at the same potential $V$.

$$ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q_1}{R_1} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q_2}{R_2} $$

This implies: $$ \frac{Q_1}{R_1} = \frac{Q_2}{R_2} $$

Now consider the surface charge density $\sigma = Q / (4\pi R^2)$. Substituting $Q = 4\pi R^2 \sigma$:

$$ \frac{4\pi R_1^2 \sigma_1}{R_1} = \frac{4\pi R_2^2 \sigma_2}{R_2} \implies \sigma_1 R_1 = \sigma_2 R_2 $$

Or:

$$ \sigma \propto \frac{1}{R} $$

The charge density is inversely proportional to the radius of curvature.
Small $R$ (sharp point) $\rightarrow$ Large $\sigma$ $\rightarrow$ Large $\vec{E}$.

This is why lightning rods are sharp. They create such an intense electric field at the tip that they ionize the surrounding air, providing a conductive path for the lightning to strike safely.
