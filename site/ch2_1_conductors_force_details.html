Deep Dive: Deriving Electrostatic Pressure

Why is the force on a surface charge \(\frac{1}{2}\sigma E\) and not simply \(\sigma E\)? The confusion arises because the electric field is discontinuous at the surface charge.

The Ambiguity:
  • Inside the conductor, \(\vec{E} = 0\).
  • Immediately outside, \(\vec{E} = (\sigma / \epsilon_0) \hat{n}\).
Which field exerts the force on the charge layer itself? The answer is the average of the two.

The "Patch" Argument

Consider a tiny patch of the surface containing charge \(\sigma da\). We can separate the total electric field into two components:

  1. \(\vec{E}_{patch}\): The field produced by the tiny patch itself.
  2. \(\vec{E}_{other}\): The field produced by everything else (the rest of the conductor).

The patch cannot exert a force on itself. Therefore, the force is due entirely to \(\vec{E}_{other}\).

The field from a flat patch of charge acts like an infinite sheet close up: $$ \vec{E}_{patch} = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} \hat{n} \quad (\text{above}), \quad -\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} \hat{n} \quad (\text{below}) $$

Now look at the total field limits:

$$ \vec{E}_{above} = \vec{E}_{other} + \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\hat{n} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\hat{n} $$ $$ \vec{E}_{below} = \vec{E}_{other} - \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\hat{n} = 0 $$

Solving either equation for \(\vec{E}_{other}\) gives the same result:

$$ \vec{E}_{other} = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} \hat{n} = \frac{1}{2} \vec{E}_{outside} $$

The Final Result

The force per unit area (\(f\)) is charge density times the field acting on it (\(E_{other}\)):

$$ f = \sigma E_{other} = \sigma \left( \frac{1}{2} E_{outside} \right) = \frac{1}{2} \sigma E_{outside} $$

Since \(E_{outside} = \sigma / \epsilon_0\), we can write this purely in terms of the field:

$$ f = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} E^2 $$

This confirms that the force is always outward (pulling the surface into the vacuum), creating an electrostatic pressure.
