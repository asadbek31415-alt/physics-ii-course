Deep Dive: Atomic Picture of Charge

The main Electric Field lesson gives the essential meaning of charge. This deep dive slows down the atomic picture: which particles carry charge, when an atom becomes charged, and why a whole atom or object can be treated as charged.

Keep the main idea simple: an object is charged when its positive and negative charges do not cancel. At the atomic scale we explain that imbalance using protons and electrons. At the macroscopic scale we usually replace all of those internal particles by one net charge, \(q_{\text{net}}\).

1. The Fundamental Property

Charge is a fundamental property of matter, much like mass. While mass determines how an object responds to gravity, charge determines how it interacts with electromagnetic fields.

There are two types of charge: Positive (+) and Negative (-). As Benjamin Franklin famously discovered, like charges repel, and opposite charges attract.

2. The Subatomic Particles

In the macroscopic world, charge seems continuous. But on an atomic level, it is carried by discrete particles. The atom consists of a dense nucleus containing Protons and Neutrons, surrounded by a cloud of Electrons.

Interesting Fact: Protons and Neutrons are about 1,840 times heavier than Electrons! Despite this massive difference in mass, the Proton and Electron have exactly the same magnitude of electric charge.

3. When Does an Atom Become Charged?

A normal atom is usually neutral because its number of protons equals its number of electrons. The positive charge of the nucleus and the negative charge of the electron cloud cancel:

\(q_{\text{atom}} = (+N_p e) + (-N_e e) = (N_p - N_e)e\)

Here \(N_p\) is the number of protons and \(N_e\) is the number of electrons. In the simulator below, the model atom starts with 6 protons, 6 neutrons, and 6 electrons, so its positive and negative charges cancel.

Loses an electron

If an atom loses one or more electrons, it has more protons than electrons. Its net charge is positive, so it becomes a positive ion or cation.

Gains an electron

If an atom gains one or more electrons, it has more electrons than protons. Its net charge is negative, so it becomes a negative ion or anion.

No imbalance

If the number of protons and electrons is the same, the atom can contain many charged particles internally but still have zero net charge.

Why do we say the whole atom is charged? A single electron is always negative and a single proton is always positive. But an atom is a system made from many charges. From outside the atom, what matters is the net charge left after adding all proton and electron charges. If one electron leaves, the missing negative charge makes the whole atom behave as a \(+e\) object. If one extra electron attaches, the whole atom behaves as a \(-e\) object. Treating the atom by its net charge lets us avoid tracking every internal particle when the outside electric effect is essentially the same.

Charge appears in everyday objects for the same reason: electrons are transferred by rubbing, contact, chemical bonding, strong electric fields, or other interactions. The protons stay locked in the nuclei of atoms, while electrons are comparatively easier to move. So charging usually means redistributing electrons, not creating charge from nothing.

4. Quantization and Conservation

Charge is Quantized. This means it only comes in chunks of a specific minimum size: the elementary charge \(e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \, \text{C}\). You can have \(1e\), \(2e\), or \(1,000e\), but you can't have \(1.5e\).

Charge is also Conserved. In any closed system, the total net charge remains constant. You can move electrons from one object to another, but you cannot create or destroy net charge.
