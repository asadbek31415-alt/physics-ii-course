Linear Dielectrics

In many substances, the polarization \(\vec{P}\) is proportional to the electric field. These are called linear dielectrics. While the microscopic physics can be complex, the macroscopic behavior is often simple and elegant.

1. Susceptibility and Permittivity

For a linear dielectric, the polarization is proportional to the total macroscopic field \(\vec{E}\). We define this relationship using the electric susceptibility \(\chi_e\):

$$ \vec{P} = \epsilon_0 \chi_e \vec{E} $$

Substituting this into the definition of Displacement (\(\vec{D} = \epsilon_0 \vec{E} + \vec{P}\)), we find that \(\vec{D}\) is also proportional to \(\vec{E}\):

$$ \vec{D} = \epsilon \vec{E} $$
  1. Electric Susceptibility (\(\chi_e\)) A dimensionless measure of how easily a material polarizes. For vacuum, \(\chi_e = 0\).
  2. Permittivity (\(\epsilon\)) Defined as \(\epsilon = \epsilon_0 (1 + \chi_e)\). This determines the field strength inside the material.
  3. Dielectric Constant (\(\epsilon_r\)) The relative permittivity: \(\epsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e = \epsilon / \epsilon_0\). This is the factor by which the field is reduced.

2. Boundary Value Problems

When dealing with linear dielectrics, we often want to find the potential \(V\). If there is no free charge embedded in the dielectric (\(\rho_f = 0\)), the divergence of \(\vec{D}\) is zero.

This implies that inside a homogeneous linear dielectric, the potential obeys Laplace's Equation:

$$ \nabla^2 V = 0 $$

This allows us to use standard techniques (like separation of variables) to solve for the field, provided we apply the correct boundary conditions at the interface between materials.

3. Forces on Dielectrics

A neutral dielectric slab is attracted into the gap of a charged capacitor. This phenomenon cannot be explained by the uniform field inside the capacitor, as that field is perpendicular to the motion.

Instead, the force arises from the fringing fields at the edges. These non-uniform fields pull the induced dipoles inward to regions of stronger field, lowering the total energy of the system.

$$ F = \frac{1}{2} V^2 \frac{dC}{dx} $$
