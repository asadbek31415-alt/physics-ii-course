Deep Dive: Coulomb's Law

This deep dive keeps the same geometry as the main lesson and focuses on how Coulomb's law gives a vector force, not just a number.

Notation and idea. The source charge \(q\) sits at \(\vec{r}'\), and the test charge \(Q\) sits at \(\vec{r}\). The separation vector \(\vecrsep=\vec{r}-\vec{r}'\) points from the source to the test charge; its length is \(\rsep=|\vecrsep|\), and its direction-only version is \(\hatrsep=\vecrsep/\rsep\). In Coulomb's law, the scalar factor \(\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{qQ}{\rsep^2}\) sets the force size and sign, while \(\hatrsep\) supplies the direction. Multiplying them gives the vector force \(\vec{F}_Q\).

Physical Limits & Behavior

The Idea of Proportionality

Coulomb's Law is more than just a calculation; it is a statement of proportionality. Specifically, it tells us that:

  • The force is jointly proportional to the product of the charges ($F \propto qQ$). Doubling one charge doubles the force; doubling both quadruples it.
  • The force is inversely proportional to the square of the separation length ($F \propto 1/\rsep^2$). This "Inverse Square Law" is a hallmark of physical phenomena that spread out from a point source into three-dimensional space.

The sign of the product also gives the direction. If the charges have the same sign, then \(qQ > 0\), so the force points in the same direction as \(\hatrsep\). Since \(\hatrsep\) points from the source charge toward the test charge, the test charge is pushed away: like charges repel. If the signs are opposite, then \(qQ < 0\), so the force reverses direction and the charges attract.

$$ \vec{F}_Q = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{qQ}{\rsep^2}\hatrsep $$

What happens as $\rsep \to \infty$?

As the distance between the charges increases, the denominator $\rsep^2$ grows very large, causing the force $F$ to approach zero. This aligns with our intuition: objects extremely far away from each other have negligible influence. We say the electrostatic force is "long-range" (it never technically hits zero), but effectively vanishes at great distances.

What happens as $\rsep \to 0$?

Mathematically, if $\rsep$ approaches zero, the force approaches infinity ($F \to \infty$). This is a singularity. In classical electrostatics, we treat charges as "point particles" with zero size, which leads to this mathematical explosion.

Concept Check: Self-Force. Coulomb's Law ($F = k \frac{qQ}{\rsep^2}$) fundamentally describes an interaction. It requires two parties: a source ($q$) and a responder ($Q$). A single isolated charge creates a potential for force (an electric field), but it cannot exert a force on itself. No second charge means no force.

Worked Vector Calculation

Let the source charge be \(q = +2.0\,\mu\text{C}\) at \(\vec{r}'=(1,1,0)\,\text{m}\). Put the test charge \(Q=-3.0\,\mu\text{C}\) at \(\vec{r}=(4,5,0)\,\text{m}\).

1. Separation

Build the vector from source to test:

$$ \begin{aligned} \vecrsep&=\vec{r}-\vec{r}'\\ &=(4-1,\,5-1,\,0-0)\\ &=(3,4,0)\,\text{m} \end{aligned} $$ $$ \rsep=|\vecrsep|=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\,\text{m} $$ $$ \hatrsep=\left(\frac{3}{5},\frac{4}{5},0\right) $$ $$ \hatrsep=(0.60,0.80,0) $$

2. Magnitude scale

Use Coulomb's constant \(k=8.99\times10^9\):

$$ \begin{aligned} qQ&=(+2.0)(-3.0)\times10^{-12}\\ &=-6.0\times10^{-12}\,\text{C}^2 \end{aligned} $$ $$ \begin{aligned} k\frac{qQ}{\rsep^2} &=(8.99\times10^9) \frac{-6.0\times10^{-12}}{5^2}\\ &=-2.16\times10^{-3}\,\text{N} \end{aligned} $$

3. Force vector

Attach the unit vector:

$$ \vec{F}_Q=\left(k\frac{qQ}{\rsep^2}\right)\hatrsep $$ $$ \vec{F}_Q=(-2.16\times10^{-3})(0.60,0.80,0) $$ $$ \vec{F}_Q=(-1.30,\,-1.73,\,0)\times10^{-3}\,\text{N} $$

The force magnitude is \(2.16\times10^{-3}\,\text{N}\). Because \(qQ<0\), the force points opposite \(\hatrsep\): back toward the positive source charge at \(\vec{r}'\).

The Constants: Why $4\pi$?

You will often see the proportionality constant written in two ways. In introductory physics, we often use $k$. In advanced electromagnetism, we strictly use $\epsilon_0$.

$$ k \approx 9 \times 10^9 \, \frac{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}{\text{C}^2} \quad \equiv \quad \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} $$

The Geometry of Space

Why include the factor $4\pi$? This is a deliberate choice called rationalization.

The electric field spreads out spherically from a point charge. The surface area of a sphere is $4\pi \rsep^2$. By putting the $4\pi$ in the denominator of Coulomb's Law, we ensure that it cancels out in Maxwell's Equations (specifically Gauss's Law), which are the fundamental laws of electromagnetism.

If we didn't put $4\pi$ here, it would appear in every single equation involving derivatives and integrals of fields later in the course. We choose to have the "ugly" factor in the algebraic formula (Coulomb's Law) to keep the calculus formulas (Maxwell's Equations) clean.

$\epsilon_0$: The Permittivity of Free Space

$$ \epsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \, \frac{\text{C}^2}{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2} $$

This constant, $\epsilon_0$ (epsilon-nought), represents the capability of a vacuum to permit electric field lines. It is essentially a scaling factor that translates our arbitrary human units of force (Newtons) and distance (meters) into the physical reality of electric charge (Coulombs).
