Deep Dive: Cavities in Conductors

What happens when a charge is placed inside a hollow cavity within a conductor? This seemingly simple question leads to profound insights about electrostatic shielding and forms the basis of the Faraday Cage.

1. Charge Inside a Cavity

Consider a neutral conductor of arbitrary shape containing a hollow cavity. We place a point charge $+q$ somewhere inside this cavity. Since the material is a conductor, the electric field in the bulk (the "meat" of the conductor) must be zero.

The Key Insight

Nature abhors a field inside a conductor. The conductor will do whatever is necessary—moving charges to its surfaces—to cancel out any internal field.

Finding the Induced Charge on the Inner Wall

Draw a Gaussian surface $\mathcal{S}$ completely within the conductor material, enclosing the cavity.

$$ \oint_{\mathcal{S}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0} $$

Since $\vec{E}=0$ everywhere on $\mathcal{S}$ (it's inside the conductor), the flux is zero. Therefore, $Q_{\text{enc}} = 0$. The enclosed charge consists of the point charge $q$ plus the induced charge on the inner wall $q_{\text{inner}}$:

$$ q + q_{\text{inner}} = 0 \implies q_{\text{inner}} = -q $$

The Outer Surface

If the conductor was originally neutral, charge is conserved. If $-q$ moved to the inner wall, a charge of $+q$ must remain. Since no charge can exist in the bulk, this $+q$ resides on the outer surface.

2. The Crucial Asymmetry

There's a beautiful asymmetry between the inner and outer surfaces:

Inner Surface

The distribution of $-q$ on the inner wall depends on where $+q$ is located inside the cavity. Move the charge, and the induced distribution shifts.

Outer Surface

The distribution of $+q$ on the outer surface does not depend on where $+q$ is located inside. It only depends on the outer geometry. The outside world has no idea where the charge is inside the cavity!

This is the principle behind electrostatic shielding. A conducting shell shields the outside from knowledge of what's happening inside.

3. The Faraday Cage

The converse is also true: a conducting shell shields its interior from external fields. If you place a conductor with a cavity in an external electric field, the charges on the outer surface rearrange to cancel the field, but no field penetrates into the cavity.

$$ \vec{E}_{\text{inside cavity}} = 0 \quad \text{(regardless of external fields)} $$

This is why:

  • You're safe inside a car during a lightning storm (the metal body acts as a Faraday cage).
  • Sensitive electronics are housed in metal enclosures.
  • Microwave ovens have metal mesh in their doors to contain the radiation.
