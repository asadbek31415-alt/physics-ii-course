Chapter 1: The Electric Field

The fundamental problem of electrostatics is to determine the force acting on a specific charge due to a configuration of other stationary charges. This requires a precise understanding of how charges interact across space.

1. The Interaction of Two Charges

Consider a single Source Charge (\(q\)) situated at position \(\vec{r}'\) and a Test Charge (\(Q\)) situated at \(\vec{r}\).

The force exerted on the test charge is defined by Coulomb's Law:

$$ \vec{F} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q Q}{\mathcal{r}^2} \hat{\mathcal{r}} $$
\(q\) The Source Charge. The charge creating the force.
\(Q\) The Test Charge. The charge experiencing the force.
\(\epsilon_0\) Permittivity of Free Space. A physical constant \(\approx 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \, \text{C}^2/\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2\).
\(\vec{\mathcal{r}}\) The Separation Vector \(\vec{\mathcal{r}} = \vec{r} - \vec{r}'\). It points from the source location to the test location.
\(\mathcal{r}\) The magnitude (distance) between the charges.
\(\hat{\mathcal{r}}\) The unit vector defined as \(\frac{\vec{\mathcal{r}}}{|\vec{\mathcal{r}}|}\). This specifies the direction of the force purely.

2. The Principle of Superposition

In most physical situations, there is more than one source charge. Experiments confirm that the electromagnetic force obeys the Principle of Superposition.

The total force on a test charge \(Q\) is the vector sum of the individual forces exerted by each source charge \(q_i\):

$$ \vec{F}_{net} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \dots + \vec{F}_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \vec{F}_i $$

3. The Electric Field

If we examine the superposition equation, we observe that the test charge \(Q\) is a common factor in every term. We can factor it out:

$$ \vec{F} = Q \left[ \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{q_i}{\mathcal{r}_i^2} \hat{\mathcal{r}}_i \right] $$

The quantity inside the brackets depends only on the distribution of the source charges and the position in space. It is independent of the test charge. We define this vector field as the Electric Field, \(\vec{E}\).

$$ \vec{E}(\vec{r}) \equiv \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{q_i}{\mathcal{r}_i^2} \hat{\mathcal{r}}_i $$

Thus, the force on any charge \(Q\) placed in an external electric field is simply \(\vec{F} = Q\vec{E}\).

4. Continuous Charge Distributions

Macroscopic objects contain such a vast number of charge carriers that it is impractical to perform a summation. Instead, we treat the charge as being spread continuously over the object.

From Summation to Integration

We divide the charged object into infinitesimal elements \(dq\). The sum becomes an integral. To perform this integration, we must express \(dq\) in terms of geometry using Charge Density (line charge \(\lambda\), surface charge \(\sigma\), or volume charge \(\rho\)).

5. The General Integral Formulation

Combining the concept of the electric field with continuous charge densities, the Electric Field at a position \(\vec{r}\) generated by a continuous volume of charge is:

$$ \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_V \frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{\mathcal{r}^2} \hat{\mathcal{r}} \, d\tau' $$

Note on vectors in integrals: The unit vector \(\hat{\mathcal{r}}\) depends on the position of the source element \(dq\). As you integrate over the volume, \(\hat{\mathcal{r}}\) changes direction. Therefore, it cannot be treated as a constant and must remain inside the integral.
