Deep Dive: Derivation of the Dipole Potential & Field

Here we derive the approximate potential \(V\) and electric field \(\vec{E}\) for a physical dipole consisting of two opposite charges \(+q\) and \(-q\) separated by a distance \(d\), observed at a large distance \(r \gg d\).

Interact: Drag point P to see how r, r+, and r- change.

1. The Exact Potential

By the principle of superposition, the potential at point \(P\) is simply the sum of the potentials from the positive and negative charges:

$$ V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left( \frac{q}{r_+} - \frac{q}{r_-} \right) $$

2. Geometry (Law of Cosines)

We need to express \(r_+\) and \(r_-\) in terms of the coordinates \(r\) and \(\theta\). Using the Law of Cosines on the triangles formed by the origin, the charge, and point P:

$$ r_{\pm}^2 = r^2 + \left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2 \mp 2r\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\cos\theta $$ $$ r_{\pm}^2 = r^2 \left( 1 \mp \frac{d}{r}\cos\theta + \frac{d^2}{4r^2} \right) $$

3. The Approximation (Multipole Expansion)

Since we assume we are far away (\(r \gg d\)), the term \(d^2/4r^2\) is negligible. We neglect it for the "pure" dipole approximation.

$$ r_{\pm} \approx r \left( 1 \mp \frac{d}{r}\cos\theta \right)^{1/2} $$ $$ \frac{1}{r_{\pm}} \approx \frac{1}{r} \left( 1 \mp \frac{d}{r}\cos\theta \right)^{-1/2} $$

Using the binomial expansion \((1+x)^n \approx 1 + nx\) for small \(x\):

$$ \frac{1}{r_{\pm}} \approx \frac{1}{r} \left( 1 \pm \frac{d}{2r}\cos\theta \right) $$

4. Substituting Back into Potential

Now we plug these approximations back into the original superposition equation:

$$ V \approx \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} \left[ \left( 1 + \frac{d}{2r}\cos\theta \right) - \left( 1 - \frac{d}{2r}\cos\theta \right) \right] $$ $$ V \approx \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r} \left( \frac{d}{r}\cos\theta \right) $$ $$ V_{dip}(r, \theta) = \frac{qd \cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} $$

Since the Dipole Moment is defined as \(p = qd\), and noting that \(\vec{p} \cdot \rhat = p \cos\theta\), we get the coordinate-independent form:

$$ V_{dip}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\vec{p} \cdot \rhat}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} $$

5. Finding the Electric Field

To find the field, we take the negative gradient in spherical coordinates: \( \vec{E} = -\nabla V = - \left( \frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\rhat + \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial \theta}\thetahat \right) \).

Radial Component:
$$ \frac{\partial V}{\partial r} = \frac{p \cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{d}{dr}(r^{-2}) = \frac{-2p \cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} $$

Angular Component:
$$ \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial V}{\partial \theta} = \frac{1}{r} \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} \frac{d}{d\theta}(\cos\theta) = \frac{-p \sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} $$

Combining these (and remembering the negative sign from \(-\nabla V\)):

$$ \vec{E}_{dip} = \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} (2\cos\theta \,\rhat + \sin\theta \,\thetahat) $$

Note The field falls off as \(1/r^3\), which is faster than a point charge (\(1/r^2\)).
