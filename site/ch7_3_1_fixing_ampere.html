Deep Dive: How Maxwell Fixed Ampère's Law

1. The Mathematical Contradiction

Before Maxwell, Ampère's Law was written as $\curl \vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{J}$. While this worked perfectly for steady currents (magnetostatics), Maxwell noticed a fatal mathematical flaw for time-varying currents.

There is a vector calculus identity that states the divergence of a curl is always zero:

$$ \div (\curl \vec{A}) = 0 $$

If we apply the divergence to both sides of the old Ampère's Law:

Left Side: $\div (\curl \vec{B}) = 0$ (Always)

Right Side: $\div (\mu_0 \vec{J}) = \mu_0 (\div \vec{J})$

However, the Continuity Equation (conservation of charge) states that $\div \vec{J} = -\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t}$. If charge density is changing (like a charging capacitor), the right side is not zero.

Conclusion: Ampère's Law $\curl \vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{J}$ is mathematically impossible if charge density varies with time.

2. Maxwell's Correction

Maxwell needed to add a term to the right side such that the divergence would essentially "cancel out" the continuity equation.

We know from Gauss's Law that $\rho = \epsilon_0 (\div \vec{E})$. We can substitute this into the Continuity Equation:

$$ \begin{align} \div \vec{J} &= -\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} \\ &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t} (\epsilon_0 \div \vec{E}) \\ &= - \div \left( \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} \right) \end{align} $$

Moving everything to one side:

$$ \div \left( \vec{J} + \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} \right) = 0 $$

This quantity inside the parentheses has a divergence of zero, which is exactly what is required to be the source of a curl. Thus, Maxwell amended Ampère's Law to:

$$ \curl \vec{B} = \mu_0 \vec{J} + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} $$

3. The Displacement Current

Maxwell called this extra term the Displacement Current Density:

$$ \vec{J}_d \equiv \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} $$

It is not a current of real charge carriers, but it has the same units as current density and creates a magnetic field just like real current does. This leads to the profound realization that a changing electric field induces a magnetic field.

Example: The Capacitor Paradox

Consider a wire carrying current $I$ charging a capacitor. Imagine an "Amperian Loop" (a circle) around the wire just before the capacitor plate.

According to the integral form $\oint \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I_{enc}$, the answer depends on which surface spans the loop:

Surface 1: Flat Disk

The flat surface cuts through the wire.

$I_{enc} = I$ (Conduction Current)

Surface 2: Balloon Shape

The surface bulges out and passes between the capacitor plates.

$I_{enc} = 0$ (No wire touches it!)

The Solution: Between the plates, there is no wire $I$, but there is a changing Electric Field $\vec{E}$.

The electric field between plates of area $A$ is $E = \sigma/\epsilon_0 = Q/(\epsilon_0 A)$. The flux is $\Phi_E = EA = Q/\epsilon_0$.

$$ I_d = \epsilon_0 \frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \epsilon_0 \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}\right) = \frac{dQ}{dt} = I $$

The displacement current $I_d$ passing through the "Balloon" surface is exactly equal to the conduction current $I$ passing through the "Flat" surface. Ampère's Law is consistent again.
