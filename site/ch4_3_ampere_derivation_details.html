Deep Dive: Deriving Ampère’s Law for H

The motivation for introducing \(\mathbf{H}\) is to express magnetic fields in terms of Free Current (\(\mathbf{J}_f\)) alone, hiding the complex bound currents (\(\mathbf{J}_b\)) inside the material definition.

1. The Differential Derivation

We start with the fundamental Ampère’s Law for the total magnetic field \(\mathbf{B}\), which depends on all currents.

Step 1: Total Current

The total current density \(\mathbf{J}\) is the sum of the free current (in wires) and bound current (from magnetization).

$$ \frac{1}{\mu_0} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{J} = \mathbf{J}_f + \mathbf{J}_b $$
Step 2: Substitute Bound Current

Recall from the previous section that the bound current is the curl of the magnetization: \(\mathbf{J}_b = \nabla \times \mathbf{M}\).

$$ \frac{1}{\mu_0} (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{J}_f + (\nabla \times \mathbf{M}) $$
Step 3: Definition of H

Move the magnetization term to the left side and group the curls:

$$ \nabla \times \left( \frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{M} \right) = \mathbf{J}_f $$

The term in parentheses is defined as the Auxiliary Field, \(\mathbf{H}\), leading to:

$$ \nabla \times \mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}_f $$

2. The Integral Form

In practice, we rarely use the differential form directly. By applying Stokes' Theorem to a surface \(S\) bounded by a loop \(P\), we get the useful form for solving problems with symmetry:

$$ \oint \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{f_{\text{enc}}} $$
Note: This integral form is typically useful only in cases of high symmetry (such as infinite cylinders, solenoids, or planes) where \(\mathbf{H}\) can be pulled out of the integral.

3. Worked Example: Long Copper Rod

Problem: A long copper rod of radius \(R\) carries a uniformly distributed (free) current \(I\). Find \(\mathbf{H}\) inside and outside the rod.

Symmetry Argument

Because the current is longitudinal (along the rod), the fields \(\mathbf{B}\), \(\mathbf{M}\), and therefore \(\mathbf{H}\) are circumferential. We apply Ampère's law to a circular loop of radius \(s\).

$$ \oint \mathbf{H} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = H(2\pi s) = I_{f_{\text{enc}}} $$
Region 1: Inside the Wire (\(s \le R\))

The enclosed free current is proportional to the area of the loop relative to the total cross-section.

$$ I_{f_{\text{enc}}} = I \frac{\pi s^2}{\pi R^2} = I \left( \frac{s}{R} \right)^2 $$

Solving for \(H\):

$$ H(2\pi s) = I \frac{s^2}{R^2} \implies \mathbf{H} = \frac{I s}{2\pi R^2} \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} $$
Region 2: Outside the Wire (\(s \ge R\))

The loop encloses the total current \(I\).

$$ H(2\pi s) = I \implies \mathbf{H} = \frac{I}{2\pi s} \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} $$
Conclusion regarding B

Outside the wire (in empty space), magnetization \(\mathbf{M} = 0\), so we can easily find \(\mathbf{B}\):

$$ \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{H} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi s} \hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} $$

Inside the wire, \(\mathbf{B}\) cannot be determined yet because we do not know \(\mathbf{M}\) (though in copper it is very small).
