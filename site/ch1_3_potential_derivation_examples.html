Deep Dive: Calculations & Examples

There are two primary ways to calculate the potential \(V\):

  1. Method 1: Integrate the Electric Field (if \(\vec{E}\) is already known, e.g., via Gauss's Law).
  2. Method 2: Integrate the Charge Distribution (if \(\vec{E}\) is unknown).

1. The Master Formulas

If you know the Field \(\vec{E}\):

$$ V(\vec{r}) = - \int_{\infty}^{\vec{r}} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} $$

If you know the Charge Density \(\rho\):

$$ V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int \frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|} d\tau' $$

2. Example: Spherical Shell

Consider a spherical shell of radius \(R\) with uniform surface charge \(\sigma\). We want to find the potential everywhere.

Method 1: Integrating the Field

From Gauss's Law, we know the field is:
Outside (\(r > R\)): \(\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r^2} \hat{r}\)
Inside (\(r < R\)): \(\vec{E} = 0\)

Step A: Outside

For a point \(r > R\), we integrate from infinity:

$$ V(r) = - \int_{\infty}^{r} \left( \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{z^2} \right) dz = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{r} $$

This is identical to the potential of a point charge.

Step B: Inside

For a point \(r < R\), we must integrate from infinity to \(R\), and then from \(R\) to \(r\):

$$ V(r) = - \int_{\infty}^{R} \vec{E}_{\text{out}} \cdot d\vec{l} - \int_{R}^{r} \vec{E}_{\text{in}} \cdot d\vec{l} $$

The first term gives \(V(R)\). The second term is zero because \(\vec{E}_{\text{in}} = 0\).

$$ V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q}{R} \quad (\text{Constant!}) $$

Key Insight: The potential inside is not zero. It is constant. It takes work to bring a charge from infinity to the surface \(R\), but no additional work to move it around inside the shell.

3. Example: Uniform Line Charge

Consider a thin rod of length \(2L\) lying on the x-axis, with uniform charge density \(\lambda\). Let's find the potential at a distance \(z\) on the z-axis (perpendicular to the rod).

Method 2: Integrating Charge Density

Here, we chop the rod into bits \(dq = \lambda dx\). The distance from a chunk at \(x\) to the point \(P\) on the axis is \(\rcurs = \sqrt{z^2 + x^2}\).

$$ V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_{-L}^{L} \frac{\lambda dx}{\sqrt{z^2 + x^2}} $$

Evaluating this integral gives:

$$ V = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left[ \ln(x + \sqrt{z^2 + x^2}) \right]_{-L}^{L} $$ $$ V = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0} \ln \left( \frac{L + \sqrt{z^2 + L^2}}{z} \right) $$

This method is often "brute force" calculus, but it works even when there is no symmetry for Gauss's Law.

4. Summary: The Electrostatic Triangle

In electrostatics, we deal with three fundamental quantities: the source charge distribution (\(\rho\)), the electric field (\(\vec{E}\)), and the electric potential (\(V\)). We have derived six formulas that allow us to translate any one of these quantities into any other.

Strategy: Which Path Should I Take?

While you can theoretically calculate any quantity from any other, the difficulty of the math varies wildly depending on the "path" you choose.

Scenario A: High Symmetry

If the problem has Spherical, Cylindrical, or Planar symmetry:
Path: \(\rho \rightarrow \vec{E} \rightarrow V\)

  • Step 1: Use Gauss's Law to find \(\vec{E}\) easily.
  • Step 2: Integrate \(\vec{E}\) (line integral) to find \(V\).

Scenario B: Low Symmetry (General Case)

If the charge distribution is "ugly" or lacks symmetry, the direct vector integral for \(\vec{E}\) is difficult because you must track components (\(\hat{x}, \hat{y}, \hat{z}\)).
Path: \(\rho \rightarrow V \rightarrow \vec{E}\)

  • Step 1: Calculate \(V\) first using the integral formula. Since \(V\) is a scalar, you just add numbers, not vectors.
  • Step 2: Calculate \(\vec{E}\) by taking the Gradient (\(\vec{E} = -\nabla V\)). Derivatives are easier than integrals!
$$ \text{Poisson's Eq: } \nabla^2 V = -\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} $$

(Directly relates Source \(\rho\) to Potential \(V\))
