Deep Dive: The Copper Rod

This example demonstrates how to find the auxiliary field $\bfH$ in a simple geometric configuration where free current is distributed throughout a material. Since $\bfH$ depends only on the free current, it simplifies calculations immensely.

The Problem:

A long copper rod of radius $R$ carries a uniformly distributed (free) current $I$. Find $\bfH$ inside and outside the rod.

Solution

Copper is weakly diamagnetic, so the dipoles will line up opposite to the field. This results in bound currents running antiparallel to $I$ within the wire, and parallel to $I$ along the surface. However, to calculate $\bfH$, we only need to consider the free current.

Inside the Wire ($s \le R$)

We use a circular Amperian loop of radius $s$. The enclosed free current is proportional to the area:

$$ H(2\pi s) = I_{f,enc} = I \frac{\pi s^2}{\pi R^2} $$
$$ \bfH = \frac{I}{2\pi R^2} s \bfphi $$

Outside the Wire ($s \ge R$)

The total free current enclosed is simply $I$:

$$ \bfH = \frac{I}{2\pi s} \bfphi $$

Note on B: Outside the wire (in empty space), $\bfM = 0$, so $\bfB = \mu_0 \bfH$. Inside the wire, $\bfB$ cannot be determined at this stage without knowing the magnetization properties of copper.

Why Use H instead of B?

In the laboratory, $\bfH$ is often a more useful quantity than $\bfB$ for a very practical reason: Current.

  • To build an electromagnet, you run a certain (free) current through a coil. This current is what you read on your dial, and it directly determines $\bfH$ (specifically, the line integral of $\bfH$).
  • $\bfB$ depends on the specific materials used (like iron cores) and even on the history of the magnet (hysteresis).

This is the opposite of the situation in electrostatics, where Voltage controls $\bfE$, making $\bfD$ less common in manual laboratory adjustments.
