Deep Dive: Energy in Dielectric Systems

When we calculate the energy of a configuration involving dielectrics, we must be careful. Are we counting the work done on all charges, or just the free charges we can physically manipulate?

1. The Capacitor Motivation

Recall that the energy of a charged capacitor is:

$$ W = \frac{1}{2} C V^2 $$

When a linear dielectric is inserted, the capacitance increases: \(C = \epsilon_r C_{\text{vac}}\). This means for a fixed voltage, the energy stored increases by a factor of \(\epsilon_r\).

Physically, this is because we must add more free charge to maintain the same potential, as some of the field is canceled by the bound charges of the dielectric.

2. The Incremental Work Proof

Suppose the dielectric is fixed in place, and we build up the free charge little by little. At any moment, adding a small extra amount of free charge \(\delta \rho_f\) at potential \(V\) requires work

$$ \delta W = \int V\,\delta \rho_f \, d\tau $$

Gauss's law for the displacement field says \(\div \vec{D} = \rho_f\), so the added free charge is \(\delta \rho_f = \div(\delta \vec{D})\).

$$ \delta W = \int V\,\div(\delta \vec{D})\,d\tau $$

Moving the derivative from \(\delta \vec{D}\) onto \(V\), and using \(\vec{E} = -\grad V\), gives

$$ \delta W = \int \vec{E}\cdot\delta\vec{D}\,d\tau $$

The boundary term is zero if we integrate over all space, because the fields die away far from the system.

Now use the fact that the material is linear:

$$ \vec{D} = \epsilon \vec{E} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec{E} = \frac{\vec{D}}{\epsilon} $$

Let the final fields be \(\vec{D}\) and \(\vec{E}\). While we build the system, imagine turning them on gradually. At the fraction \(\lambda\) of the final charge,

$$ \vec{D}_\lambda = \lambda \vec{D}, \qquad \vec{E}_\lambda = \lambda \vec{E}, \qquad d\vec{D}_\lambda = \vec{D}\,d\lambda $$

Therefore the energy density is

$$ w = \int_0^1 \vec{E}_\lambda \cdot d\vec{D}_\lambda = \int_0^1 (\lambda \vec{E})\cdot(\vec{D}\,d\lambda) = \frac{1}{2}\vec{D}\cdot\vec{E} $$

3. The Final Formula

Adding this energy density over all space gives:

$$ W = \frac{1}{2} \int \vec{D} \cdot \vec{E} \, d\tau $$

For linear dielectrics, this can also be written in terms of the permittivity: $$ W = \frac{\epsilon}{2} \int E^2 \, d\tau $$

4. What does this energy include?

There are two ways to think about "assembling" a system with dielectrics:

  1. Counting Every Charge: If you move every single charge (free and bound) into place one by one, you use the vacuum formula \(W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} \int E^2 \, d\tau\). This counts only the field energy.
  2. Counting Physical Work: If you bring in the free charges and let the dielectric respond naturally, you use the formula above. This counts the field energy plus the energy stored in the "stretched" molecules of the dielectric.

In most engineering and physics problems, we are interested in the total work done on the free charges, so the \(\vec{D} \cdot \vec{E}\) form is the one we use.
