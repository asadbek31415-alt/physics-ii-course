Deep Dive: Batteries & Sources

An electromotive force ($\mathcal{E}$) is not actually a "force" but an energy per unit charge. It is the work done by non-electrostatic forces to move charge against the electric field within a source.

The Nature of Source Force

Inside a battery, chemical forces drive positive charges from the negative terminal to the positive terminal, opposing the electric field $\vec{E}$ that points from (+) to (-).

If $\vec{f}_s$ is the force per unit charge due to chemical reactions, the total force is $\vec{f} = \vec{f}_s + \vec{E}$. In an open circuit, the current is zero, so the net force is zero ($\vec{E} = -\vec{f}_s$). The potential difference across the terminals is:

$$ V = - \int_{a}^{b} \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} = \int_{a}^{b} \vec{f}_s \cdot d\vec{l} \equiv \mathcal{E} $$

So for an ideal source with no current, the voltage equals the emf.

Ideal vs. Real Batteries

Real batteries have resistance to the flow of ions internally. We model this as an ideal emf $\mathcal{E}$ in series with an internal resistance $r$.

When a current $I$ flows, the terminal voltage $V$ decreases due to the internal potential drop:

$$ V_{term} = \mathcal{E} - I r $$

This explains why a battery's output voltage decreases when connected to a "heavy" load: as you draw more current, more of the battery's energy is "wasted" internally just to get the charges through its own internal resistance ($r$).

Maximum Power Transfer Theorem

A fundamental question in circuit design is: what load resistance $R$ will draw the most power from a real source?

Using the real battery model, the current in the circuit is $I = \mathcal{E} / (R + r)$. The power delivered to the load is:

$$ P = I^2 R = \left( \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R + r} \right)^2 R $$

To find the maximum, we take the derivative with respect to $R$ and set it to zero:

$$ \frac{dP}{dR} = \mathcal{E}^2 \left[ \frac{(R+r)^2 - 2R(R+r)}{(R+r)^4} \right] = 0 \implies (R+r) - 2R = 0 $$

This results in the Matching Condition:

The maximum power is delivered to the load when:

$$ R = r $$

Efficiency vs. Power

It is vital to distinguish between maximum power and maximum efficiency. The efficiency $\eta$ is the ratio of power delivered to the load vs. total power produced:

$$ \eta = \frac{P_{load}}{P_{total}} = \frac{I^2 R}{I^2 (R + r)} = \frac{R}{R + r} $$
  • At $R = r$ (Max Power): Efficiency is only 50%. Half the energy is lost as heat inside the battery.
  • As $R \to \infty$ (Max Efficiency): Efficiency approaches 100%, but the actual power delivered drops to zero because the current vanishes.

In power grids, we prioritize efficiency ($R \gg r$), but in communications and audio (impedance matching), we prioritize maximum power transfer ($R = r$).
