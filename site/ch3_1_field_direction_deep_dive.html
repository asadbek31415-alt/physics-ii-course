Deep Dive: The Field's Direction

A straight current does not make a magnetic field that points toward or away from the wire. It makes a field that wraps around the wire. This page is about turning that fact into a usable direction rule, not just memorizing a picture.

The reason is the Lorentz force: $\vec{F} = q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$. To find the magnetic force on moving charge, you need both the charge velocity $\vec{v}$ and the local magnetic field $\vec{B}$. When $\vec{B}$ circles around the source wire, the force on a nearby current-carrying wire points sideways, not along the current.

Use the right-hand rule as a direction tool: point your thumb along the current, and your fingers curl in the direction of $\vec{B}$. The field direction is tangent to the circular path at each point.

Compass Check

A compass near a current-carrying wire turns until its needle lies tangent to the local magnetic-field path. As you move around the wire, the compass direction changes because the tangent direction changes. The Paradox of Attraction

The two-wire result can feel surprising: if the magnetic field circles around a wire, how can two straight wires attract or repel each other?

Focus on one wire as the source. It creates a circular $\vec{B}$ field. At the position of the second wire, the second wire samples just the local value of that circular field: into the screen on one side, out of the screen on the other.

Now apply $\vec{F} = q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ to the moving charges in the second wire. The velocity is along the wire, while $\vec{B}$ points into or out of the screen, so the force is sideways. For same-direction currents, that sideways force points inward, so the wires attract. Reverse one current, and the cross product reverses, so the wires repel.

Direction chain: source current $\rightarrow$ circular $\vec{B}$ direction; moving charges in the second wire $\rightarrow$ $\vec{v}$; cross product $\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$ $\rightarrow$ sideways force.
