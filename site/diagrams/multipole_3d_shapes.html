Multipole Expansion

When you look at a complex blob of charge from very far away, you don't see the details. You see a simplified version. The Multipole Expansion is the mathematical tool we use to organize these simplifications into a hierarchy of shapes.

1. The Hierarchy of Shapes

What exactly is a "Dipole" or a "Quadrupole"? They are specific arrangements of charge that result in different levels of cancellation.

1. Monopole (n=0) Total Charge \(Q \neq 0\)

The simplest object. It is just a net amount of charge. It looks like a single point.
Example: A proton.

2. Dipole (n=1) \(Q=0\), but separated

If you take a (+) and a (-) and pull them apart, the net charge is zero, but they don't perfectly cancel. This separation creates a "Dipole Moment" \(\vec{p}\).
Example: A water molecule.

3. Quadrupole (n=2) \(Q=0, \vec{p}=0\)

If you take two opposing dipoles and push them together, even the dipole moment cancels. What's left is a "Quadrupole" (four poles).

2. Why do we need this?

Calculating the exact potential of a messy object involves a difficult integral. However, if we are far away (\(r \gg \text{size of object}\)), we can approximate the potential as a sum of these poles.

Because higher poles involve more cancellation (positive charges screening negative charges), their fields die off much faster.

$$ V(\vec{r}) \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left( \frac{Q}{r} + \frac{\vec{p} \cdot \hat{r}}{r^2} + \frac{1}{r^3}(\dots) \right) $$
  • Monopole Term (\(1/r\)): Dominates if the object has a net charge.
  • Dipole Term (\(1/r^2\)): Dominates if the object is neutral (like an atom).
  • Quadrupole Term (\(1/r^3\)): Dominates if the object is neutral and symmetric.

3. The Electric Field of a Dipole

Since the potential \(V\) of a dipole falls off as \(1/r^2\), the Electric Field (\(\vec{E} = -\nabla V\)) falls off as \(1/r^3\). This is a very short-range field compared to a point charge.

Spherical Coordinates

If we align the dipole \(\vec{p}\) with the z-axis, the field is:

$$ \vec{E}_{\text{dip}}(r, \theta) = \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} (2\cos\theta \hat{r} + \sin\theta \hat{\theta}) $$

Notice the structure:

  • Along the axis (\(\theta=0\)), the field points along \(\hat{r}\) (parallel to \(\vec{p}\)).
  • At the equator (\(\theta=90^\circ\)), the field points along \(-\hat{\theta}\) (anti-parallel to \(\vec{p}\)).
