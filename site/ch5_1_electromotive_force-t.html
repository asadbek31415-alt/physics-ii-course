Electromotive Force

In Electrostatics, charges were stationary, and the electric field inside a conductor was zero. Electrodynamics changes the rules. To make charges move continuously (a current), we must push them. This chapter explains the "Push" (EMF) and the "Drag" (Resistance).

📝 Primer: Key Definitions (Click to Expand)
$\mathbf{J}$ Current Density (Amps/m²). The flow of charge per unit area. Vector pointing in direction of flow.
$\sigma$ Conductivity. How easily a material allows flow. Large for metals, small for glass.
$\rho$ Resistivity ($1/\sigma$). How much a material resists flow.

1. The "Drag": Ohm's Law

Electrons moving through a wire are like people running through a dense crowd. They don't accelerate forever; they collide with ions and scatter. To keep them moving at a constant average speed, you need a continuous force $\mathbf{f}$.

⚠️ Major Conceptual Shift In Electrostatics, $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside a conductor.
In Electrodynamics (circuits), $\mathbf{E} \neq 0$ inside the wire! This $\mathbf{E}$ field provides the force that pushes the current.

The Microscopic Law: For many materials, the current density is proportional to the force pushing it:

$$ \mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E} $$

This simple relation implies that if you double the field $\mathbf{E}$, you double the flow $\mathbf{J}$. When applied to a wire of length $L$ and area $A$, this integrates into the macroscopic version we know from high school:

$$ V = IR \quad \text{where} \quad R = \rho \frac{L}{A} $$

2. The "Pump": Electromotive Force (EMF)

Ohm's Law tells us the wire resists flow. So, what pushes the charges? An electrostatic field alone cannot do it, because the net work done by an electrostatic field around a closed loop is always zero ($\oint \mathbf{E} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$).

To drive a steady current, we need a Source (battery, generator, photocell) that exerts a non-electrostatic force $\mathbf{f}_s$.

$$ \mathcal{E} \equiv \oint \mathbf{f}_s \cdot d\mathbf{l} $$
  • It's not a Force: Despite the name, EMF is the work per unit charge (Joules/Coulomb = Volts).
  • The Battery Role: Inside a battery, chemical forces ($\mathbf{f}_s$) push positive charges from the (-) terminal to the (+) terminal, against the electric field. It acts exactly like a water pump lifting water to a higher elevation.

Fig 2: The "Pump" (Source) does work to lift charges to high potential. The "Pipe" (Resistor) dissipates that energy as heat.

3. A Specific Source: Motional EMF

Batteries use chemicals to create the push. Generators use magnetic fields. When a conductor moves through a magnetic field, the charges inside experience the Lorentz Force:

$$ \mathbf{F}_{mag} = q(\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}) $$

This magnetic force acts as the source force $\mathbf{f}_s$. If we move a loop of wire, this force drives a current. Calculating the cross product $\mathbf{v} \times \mathbf{B}$ for every part of a complex loop is difficult, so we use a powerful shortcut called the Flux Rule for the changing magnetic flux $\Phi_B$ linked with the loop.

$$ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} $$

This rule states that the EMF is simply the rate of change of magnetic flux ($\Phi_B$) through the loop. It works whether the loop is moving, expanding, or rotating.
