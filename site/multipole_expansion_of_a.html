Deep Dive: Multipole Expansion of A

When dealing with a localized current distribution (like a loop of wire or a spinning charged sphere), calculating the exact vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ at every point in space can be incredibly difficult.

However, if we are observing the system from a distance $r$ that is much larger than the size of the current loop ($r \gg r'$), we can approximate the potential using a series expansion known as the Multipole Expansion.

The Expansion

We start with the exact integral solution for the vector potential:

\[ \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \oint \frac{\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r}')}{|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|} d\tau' \]

Using the law of cosines and the Taylor expansion for $1/|\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|$ in powers of $(r'/r)$, we can write $\mathbf{A}$ as a sum of terms:

\[ \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{A}_{\text{mon}}(\mathbf{r}) + \mathbf{A}_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf{r}) + \dots \]

1. The Monopole Term ($n=0$)

The first term in the expansion corresponds to the magnetic monopole:

\[ \mathbf{A}_{\text{mon}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r} \oint \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r}') d\tau' \]

For any steady current confined to a closed loop, the total integral of the current density vector $\oint \mathbf{J} d\tau'$ is always zero. (Think of it as the sum of current vectors going around a circle; they all cancel out).

Result: The magnetic monopole term is always zero. This confirms physically that magnetic monopoles do not exist in classical magnetostatics.

2. The Dipole Term ($n=1$)

Since the monopole term vanishes, the dominant contribution at large distances usually comes from the second term: the Magnetic Dipole.

\[ \mathbf{A}_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^2} \oint r' \cos\theta' \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r}') d\tau' \]

Through vector calculus identities, this integral can be rewritten in a much more elegant form using the Magnetic Dipole Moment, $\mathbf{m}$:

\[ \mathbf{m} \equiv \frac{1}{2} \oint (\mathbf{r}' \times \mathbf{J}) d\tau' \]

Substituting this back gives the standard formula for the dipole vector potential:

\[ \mathbf{A}_{\text{dip}}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \frac{\mathbf{m} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2} \]

Visualizing the Dipole Field

The equation $\mathbf{A} \propto \mathbf{m} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}$ tells us the geometry of the potential:

  • $\mathbf{m}$ points along the axis of the current loop (Right Hand Rule).
  • $\mathbf{A}$ is perpendicular to both $\mathbf{m}$ and the position vector $\mathbf{r}$.
  • This results in $\mathbf{A}$ "swirling" around the dipole moment axis, decaying as $1/r^2$.
