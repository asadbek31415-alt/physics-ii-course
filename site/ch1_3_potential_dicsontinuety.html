Deep Dive: Boundary Conditions

What happens to the electric potential when you cross a surface charged with density \(\sigma\)? Does it jump? Does it bend?

1. Why do we study this?

In real-world physics problems, we rarely know the charge distribution \(\rho\) everywhere. Instead, we usually have empty space (where \(\rho=0\)) bounded by charged surfaces.

To solve these problems, we solve Laplace's Equation (\(\nabla^2 V = 0\)) in the empty regions. However, differential equations have infinite solutions. To pick the correct solution, we need to know how to "stitch" the regions together at the boundaries. These rules are the Boundary Conditions.

2. The Field Discontinuity

From Gauss's Law, we know that the electric field undergoes a discontinuous jump when crossing a surface charge \(\sigma\). If we draw a "Gaussian Pillbox" across the surface, the flux tells us that the perpendicular component changes:

$$ \vec{E}_{\text{above}} - \vec{E}_{\text{below}} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} \hat{n} $$

The field physically jumps. For example, if you have a single sheet of charge, the field points up above it and down below it. This sudden reversal is a discontinuity.

(Note: We will see a crucial application of this in the next chapter when we study Conductors, where the field drops to zero instantly inside the material.)

3. The Potential Continuity

Does the potential \(V\) also jump? Let's calculate the potential difference between a point \(a\) just below and \(b\) just above the surface:

$$ V_{\text{above}} - V_{\text{below}} = - \int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{l} $$

As the distance between \(a\) and \(b\) shrinks to zero, the integral vanishes (because \(\vec{E}\) is finite, and the path length is zero). Therefore:

$$ V_{\text{above}} = V_{\text{below}} $$

Conclusion: The potential is continuous everywhere. It never "breaks" or jumps, even when crossing a charge sheet.

4. The "Kink" in the Potential

While \(V\) is continuous, its slope is not. Since \(\vec{E} = -\nabla V\), the discontinuity in \(\vec{E}\) implies a discontinuity in the gradient of \(V\).

To see exactly where the formula comes from, let's look at the normal component (perpendicular to the surface) of the field jump:

$$ E_{above}^{\perp} - E_{below}^{\perp} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} $$

The normal component of the electric field is related to the derivative of the potential along the normal direction \(\hat{n}\):

$$ E_n = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial n} $$

Substituting this into our field jump equation:

$$ \left( -\frac{\partial V_{above}}{\partial n} \right) - \left( -\frac{\partial V_{below}}{\partial n} \right) = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} $$

Simplifying the signs, we get the fundamental boundary condition for the potential gradient:

$$ \frac{\partial V_{above}}{\partial n} - \frac{\partial V_{below}}{\partial n} = -\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} $$

This means the graph of \(V\) has a sharp "kink" at the boundary. The change in slope is directly proportional to the surface charge density \(\sigma\).

Visualizing the Boundary: Notice how the Field (arrows) jumps, but the Potential (height) is continuous, forming a sharp peak or valley.
