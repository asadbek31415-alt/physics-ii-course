Deep Dive: Radiation Pressure

Momentum in Fields

Just as fields store energy, they also store momentum. This is a crucial realization: momentum is not exclusive to matter! The momentum density $\vec{g}$ (momentum per unit volume) is given by:

$$ \vec{g} = \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \vec{S} = \frac{1}{c^2} \vec{S} = \epsilon_0 (\vec{E} \times \vec{B}) $$

The total momentum stored in a volume $V$ is $\vec{p}_{field} = \int_V \vec{g} \, d\tau$. If the field configuration changes, momentum is transferred to the charges in that volume, satisfying a conservation law analogous to Poynting's Theorem.

Radiation Pressure Cases

When a wave strikes a surface, it transfers its momentum. From Newton's Second Law, the force is the rate of momentum transfer ($\vec{F} = d\vec{p}/dt$). The pressure $P$ is this force per unit area:

$P = I/c$ Perfect Absorption. The surface "stops" the light, absorbing $100\%$ of its momentum. $P = \langle g \rangle c = I/c$.
$P = 2I/c$ Perfect Reflection. The surface reverses the direction of the light. The momentum change is $\Delta p = p_{final} - p_{initial} = (-p) - p = -2p$, doubling the pressure.

Note: In most everyday scenarios, radiation pressure is incredibly weak (a few micronewtons per square meter), yet it is sufficient to push comet tails away from the sun.

Advance Concept: The Maxwell Stress Tensor

In general, the force on a surface isn't always from a simple plane wave hitting it head-on. Physicists use the Maxwell Stress Tensor ($T_{ij}$) to calculate the total force on a volume by integrating over its surface: $$ \vec{F}_{total} = \oint_S \overleftrightarrow{\mathbf{T}} \cdot d\vec{a} - \frac{d}{dt} \int_V \vec{g} \, d\tau $$ The tensor $T_{ij}$ represents the flow of momentum through the surface, describing both pressure (normal to surface) and shear stress (tangential to surface).
