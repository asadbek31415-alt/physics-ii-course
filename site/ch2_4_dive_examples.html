Deep Dive: Wire with Insulation

Problem: A long straight wire, carrying a uniform line charge \(\lambda\), is surrounded by rubber insulation out to a radius \(a\). Find the electric displacement \(\vec{D}\).

1. Applying Gauss's Law for D

The beauty of the Electric Displacement is that it depends only on free charge. The rubber insulation will become polarized and create bound charges, but we can ignore them for now.

We draw a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \(s\) and length \(L\). Because of the cylindrical symmetry, \(\vec{D}\) must point radially outward: \(\vec{D} = D(s) \hat{s}\).

Using Gauss's Law for Displacement:

$$ \oint \vec{D} \cdot d\vec{a} = Q_{f,enc} $$

The total free charge enclosed by the cylinder is simply the charge on the wire (\(\lambda L\)). The surface integral gives the area of the cylinder wall (\(2\pi s L\)):

$$ D(2\pi s L) = \lambda L $$

2. The Result

Solving for \(\vec{D}\):

$$ \vec{D} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi s} \hat{s} $$

Crucially: This formula holds everywhere—both inside the insulation (\(s < a\)) and outside it (\(s > a\)). The symmetry of the free charge dictates \(\vec{D}\) regardless of the material.

3. Finding the Electric Field

While we know \(\vec{D}\) everywhere, finding \(\vec{E}\) requires knowing the relationship between \(\vec{D}\) and \(\vec{E}\) (which depends on polarization).

Outside the Insulation (\(s > a\))

Outside, there is no material, so \(\vec{P} = 0\). Since \(\vec{D} = \epsilon_0 \vec{E} + \vec{P}\), we simply have \(\vec{D} = \epsilon_0 \vec{E}\).

$$ \vec{E} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi \epsilon_0 s} \hat{s} \quad (\text{for } s > a) $$

Inside the Insulation (\(s < a\))

Inside the rubber, \(\vec{E}\) cannot be determined yet because we do not know \(\vec{P}\).
$$ \vec{E} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}(\vec{D} - \vec{P}) $$ Without knowing the specific properties of the rubber (like its susceptibility), we stop here.

A Subtle Point: Surface Bound Charge

You might wonder: "Doesn't the polarization create a bound surface charge \(\sigma_b\) at the edge of the rubber? Why didn't we include that in our Gaussian surface?"

We did not need to include it because Gauss's Law for \(\vec{D}\) reads \(\oint \vec{D} \cdot d\vec{a} = Q_{f,enc}\). It only cares about Free Charge. The bound charge \(\sigma_b\) affects \(\vec{E}\), but it does not affect \(\vec{D}\) in this symmetry.

Furthermore, if we imagine the edge of the dielectric not as a sharp cutoff but as a thin layer where polarization tapers to zero, \(\rho_b\) varies smoothly. In this "realistic" model, there is no infinite discontinuity, and Gauss's law applies safely everywhere. The integral form protects us from these "defects" of the mathematical model.
