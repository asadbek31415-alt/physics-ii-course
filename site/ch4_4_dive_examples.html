Deep Dive: The Filled Solenoid

This example explores how a linear material alters the field of a standard solenoid. It is a fundamental calculation that demonstrates the utility of the auxiliary field $\bfH$.

The Problem:

An infinite solenoid ($n$ turns per unit length, current $I$) is filled with linear material of susceptibility $\chi_m$. Find the magnetic field inside the solenoid.

1. Finding the Auxiliary Field (H)

Because the $\bfB$ field is due in part to bound currents (which we don't yet know), we cannot compute it directly. However, for a solenoid, we can calculate $\bfH$ from the free current alone using the integral form of Ampère's law:

$$ \bfH = n I \bfz $$

2. Calculating the Total Field (B)

With $\bfH$ in hand, we apply the linear constitutive relation for magnetic media:

$$ \bfB = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m)nI \bfz $$

Physical Interpretation:

  • If the medium is paramagnetic ($\chi_m > 0$), the field is slightly enhanced.
  • If the medium is diamagnetic ($\chi_m < 0$), the field is somewhat reduced.

3. Bound Currents

The change in the field is physically caused by bound surface currents. Since the magnetization $\bfM = \chi_m \bfH = \chi_m n I \bfz$ is uniform inside, the volume bound current $\bfJ_b = \nabla \times \bfM = 0$.

The surface bound current is:

$$ \bfK_b = \bfM \times \bfn = \chi_m (\bfH \times \bfn) = \chi_m n I \bfphi $$

This bound current flows in the same direction as the free current $I$ if the material is paramagnetic ($\chi_m > 0$), and in the opposite direction if it is diamagnetic ($\chi_m < 0$).

The "Divergence Trap"

You might suppose that linear media escape the defect in the parallel between $\bfB$ and $\bfH$: since $\bfM$ and $\bfH$ are now proportional to $\bfB$, does it not follow that their divergence, like $\bfB$'s, must always vanish?

Unfortunately, it does not.

At the boundary between two materials of different permeability, the divergence of $\bfM$ can actually be infinite.

Consider the end of a cylinder of linear material. Inside, there is a uniform $\bfM$; outside, $\bfM$ is zero. If we draw a small "Gaussian pillbox" at the boundary, the flux of $\bfM$ is non-zero ($\oint \bfM \cdot d\mathbf{a} \neq 0$).

By the divergence theorem, $\nabla \cdot \bfM$ cannot vanish everywhere within it. In fact: $$ \nabla \cdot \bfH = \nabla \cdot \left( \frac{1}{\mu} \bfB \right) = \frac{1}{\mu} \nabla \cdot \bfB + \bfB \cdot \nabla \left( \frac{1}{\mu} \right) $$ So $\bfH$ is not divergenceless at points where the permeability $\mu$ is changing.

Volume vs. Surface Bound Currents

In a homogeneous linear material, the volume bound current density is proportional to the free current density:

$$ \bfJ_b = \nabla \times \bfM = \nabla \times (\chi_m \bfH) = \chi_m \bfJ_f $$

Key Takeaway: Unless free current actually flows through the material ($\bfJ_f \neq 0$), all bound current will reside on the surface.
