Deep Dive: The Drude Model

Understanding the Microscopic Origin of Resistance

Ohm's Law ($\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E}$) is not a fundamental law of nature like Maxwell’s equations. Instead, it is an empirical description of how certain materials behave. To understand why it works, we must look at the behavior of electrons at the microscopic level.

1. The Drude Model Assumptions

The Drude model treats a conductor as a 3D lattice of heavy, stationary ions with a "gas" of free electrons moving between them. It relies on a few key classical assumptions:

  • Free Electron Gas: Electrons move freely until they hit an ion.
  • Instantaneous Collisions: Collisions are sudden events that randomize the electron's velocity.
  • Mean Free Time ($\tau$): There is an average time interval between collisions, during which the electron accelerates under an external field.

2. Thermal Motion vs. Drift Velocity

Electrons are never still; they move randomly at high speeds ($v_{th} \approx 10^6$ m/s) even without a field. This random motion contributes zero net flow.

When an external field $\mathbf{E}$ is applied, it exerts a force $\mathbf{F} = -e\mathbf{E}$. Under this force, electrons constantly accelerate between collisions. However, because they are constantly colliding with the lattice—effectively resetting their velocity—they reach a steady state "average" called the drift velocity ($\mathbf{v}_d$).

3. Deriving Ohm's Law

Between collisions, an electron accelerates according to Newton's Second Law:

$$ \mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a} = -e\mathbf{E} \implies \mathbf{a} = -\frac{e\mathbf{E}}{m} $$

Starting from a randomized velocity after a collision, the electron acquires an average "extra" velocity before the next collision (time $\tau$):

$$ \mathbf{v}_d = \mathbf{a}\tau = -\frac{e\tau}{m}\mathbf{E} $$

The total current density $\mathbf{J}$ is the product of the number of electrons per volume ($n$), their charge ($-e$), and their average velocity:

$$ \mathbf{J} = n(-e)\mathbf{v}_d = n(-e) \left( -\frac{e\tau}{m}\mathbf{E} \right) = \left( \frac{ne^2\tau}{m} \right) \mathbf{E} $$
Comparing this to $\mathbf{J} = \sigma \mathbf{E}$, we find the microscopic definition of conductivity:
$$ \sigma = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m} $$

4. Macroscopic Resistance

We can bridge this to the macroscopic world for a wire of length $L$ and area $A$. Recall from the Lorentz Force Law that current is the density times area: $I = JA$. Additionally, for a uniform field, the potential difference is $V = EL$ (see Chapter 5.1).

$$ I = \sigma E A = \sigma \left(\frac{V}{L}\right) A \implies V = I \left( \frac{L}{\sigma A} \right) $$

This gives us the familiar $V = IR$, where the resistance is defined by the geometry and the material's resistivity ($\rho = 1/\sigma$): $R = \rho \frac{L}{A}$.

5. Joule Heating

Resistance exists because electrons keep losing their field-acquired kinetic energy to the lattice ions. The power dissipated per unit volume (Power Density $f$) is the rate of work done by the field:

$$ f = \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} = (\sigma \mathbf{E}) \cdot \mathbf{E} = \sigma E^2 $$

To find the total power $P$ in a wire of volume $\mathcal{V} = AL$, we integrate:

$$ P = \int f \, d\mathcal{V} = (\sigma E^2) (AL) = (\sigma E A) (EL) $$

Recognizing $I = \sigma E A$ and $V = EL$, we recover the bulk formulas:

$$ P = IV = I(IR) = I^2 R $$
