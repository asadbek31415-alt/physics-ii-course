Polarization

Matter falls into two broad classes: Conductors, where charges are free to move, and Dielectrics (insulators), where all charges are attached to specific atoms or molecules.

Even though charges in a dielectric cannot flow freely, they can shift slightly. This microscopic shifting is what we call Polarization.

1. Induced Dipoles

What happens to a neutral atom when placed in an electric field \(\vec{E}\)?

The positive nucleus is pushed in the direction of the field, while the electron cloud is pulled in the opposite direction. The atom "stretches" until the internal restoring forces balance the external electric force.

This separation of charge creates a tiny Electric Dipole Moment \(\vec{p}\), which points in the direction of \(\vec{E}\). For moderate fields, this relationship is linear:

$$ \vec{p} = \alpha \vec{E} $$

The constant \(\alpha\) is called the atomic polarizability.

2. Alignment of Polar Molecules

Some molecules, like water (\(H_2O\)), are built such that the positive and negative charges are already separated. These possess a permanent dipole moment.

When placed in an electric field, these molecules don't just stretch; they rotate. The field exerts a torque trying to line the dipole up with the field direction:

$$ \vec{\tau} = \vec{p} \times \vec{E} $$

In a material, thermal agitation constantly knocks the molecules around, fighting this alignment. The result is a partial alignment along the field.

3. The Polarization Vector (\(\vec{P}\))

Whether due to stretching atoms (induced) or rotating molecules (alignment), the result is the same: a lot of tiny dipoles pointing along the field.

To do physics on a macroscopic scale, we don't want to track billions of individual molecules. Instead, we define a vector field \(\vec{P}\), called the Polarization, defined as the dipole moment per unit volume:

$$ \vec{P} \equiv \frac{\text{dipole moment}}{\text{volume}} $$

If \(\vec{P}\) is uniform, the material is evenly polarized. If \(\vec{P}\) varies (has divergence), it implies a buildup of internal charge, which we will explore next.
