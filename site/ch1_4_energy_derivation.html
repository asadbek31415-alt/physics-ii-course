Deep Dive: Field Energy Derivation

For a continuous charge distribution, we transition from the discrete sum to a volume integral. This derivation shows how energy can be viewed as resides within the electric field itself.

1. From Sum to Integral

For a volume charge density \(\rho\), the discrete formula becomes:

$$ W = \frac{1}{2} \int \rho V d\tau $$

There is a lovely way to rewrite this result, in which \(\rho\) and \(V\) are eliminated in favor of the electric field \(\vec{E}\).

2. The Vector Calculus Path

First, we use Gauss's law to express \(\rho\) in terms of \(\vec{E}\): \(\rho = \epsilon_0 \nabla \cdot \vec{E}\).

$$ W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} \int (\nabla \cdot \vec{E}) V d\tau $$

Now use integration by parts to transfer the derivative from \(\vec{E}\) to \(V\). Using the identity \(\nabla \cdot (V\vec{E}) = V(\nabla \cdot \vec{E}) + \vec{E} \cdot (\nabla V)\):

$$ W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} \left[ -\int \vec{E} \cdot (\nabla V) d\tau + \oint V\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} \right] $$

But \(\nabla V = -\vec{E}\), so the first term simplifies:

$$ W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} \left( \int_{\mathcal{V}} E^2 d\tau + \oint_{\mathcal{S}} V\vec{E} \cdot d\vec{a} \right) $$

3. Integrating Over All Space

What volume \(\mathcal{V}\) should we integrate over? Initially, it must enclose all the charge. However, any larger volume would do just as well, since \(\rho = 0\) in the extra territory.

As we enlarge the volume, the integral of \(E^2\) can only increase (since the integrand is always positive). Evidently, the surface integral must decrease correspondingly to leave the sum intact.

  • At large distances from the charge, \(V\) goes like \(1/r\) and \(E\) like \(1/r^2\).
  • The surface area grows like \(r^2\).
  • Roughly speaking, the surface integral goes down like \(1/r\).

If we integrate over all space (\(R^3\)), the surface integral goes to zero, and we are left with:

$$ W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} \int_{\text{all space}} E^2 d\tau $$

4. Energy Density \(w_e\)

The form of the all-space integral suggests that the energy is literally distributed throughout space, with a specific amount of energy per unit volume. We define this as the electrostatic energy density (\(w_e\)):

$$ w_e = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2} E^2 $$

In this view, the total work \(W\) is simply the integral of the energy density over all space:

$$ W = \int w_e d\tau $$

This concept is central to the field theory of electromagnetism: energy is not just a bookkeeping property of charges, but a physical quantity residing in the electric field itself.
