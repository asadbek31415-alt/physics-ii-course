Deep Dive: Mutual & Self Inductance

Because the magnetic field is proportional to the current that creates it, the flux through any nearby loop must also be proportional to that current. This proportionality constant encapsulates all the complex geometry into a single number: Inductance.

1. Mutual Inductance ($M$)

Consider two loops of wire. Current $I_1$ flowing in the first loop produces a magnetic field $\vec{B}_1$. A portion of these field lines passes through the second loop, creating a magnetic flux $\Phi_{B,2}$.

Calculating $\vec{B}_1$ from the Biot-Savart law is hard. But we know one crucial fact: the field is proportional to the current. Therefore:

$$ \Phi_{B,2} = M_{21} I_1 $$

The constant $M_{21}$ is the Mutual Inductance of the two loops. It depends only on their shapes, sizes, and relative positions.

The Neumann Formula

By expressing the flux in terms of the vector potential $\vec{A}_1$, one can derive an elegant formula that makes a remarkable fact obvious:

$$ M_{21} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \oint_{loop1} \oint_{loop2} \frac{d\vec{l}_1 \cdot d\vec{l}_2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|} $$

The integral is symmetric under swapping loop 1 and loop 2. Therefore:

$$ M_{21} = M_{12} \equiv M $$

This means: the flux through loop 2 when we run current $I$ through loop 1 is identical to the flux through loop 1 when we run the same current $I$ through loop 2. We can drop the subscripts and simply call it $M$.

2. Self Inductance ($L$)

A changing current in loop 1 doesn't just induce an EMF in nearby loop 2—it also induces an EMF in itself. The flux through a loop due to its own current is:

$$ \Phi_B = L I $$

Where $L$ is the Self Inductance (or simply "inductance").

Origin of the Back EMF Formula

The formula for the back EMF comes directly from Faraday's Law. Since the induced EMF is the negative rate of change of magnetic flux:

$$ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} $$

Substituting the definition of self-inductance ($\Phi_B = LI$) into this equation:

$$ \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d(LI)}{dt} = -L \frac{dI}{dt} $$

This confirms that the Back EMF always opposes the change in current (Lenz's Law). Inductance is measured in Henries (H).

Inductance as Electrical "Mass"

The back EMF means you must "fight" an inductor to change the current. The greater the $L$, the harder it is to change the current—just like mass resists changes in velocity. This is why we call inductance "electrical inertia."

3. The LR Circuit

Consider a standard series circuit consisting of a battery $\mathcal{E}_0$, a resistor $R$, and an inductor $L$. When you close the switch, the inductor prevents the current from reaching its peak value instantly.

The total EMF is the battery minus the back EMF from the inductor. Applying Ohm's law:

$$ \mathcal{E}_0 - L\frac{dI}{dt} = IR $$

This is a first-order differential equation. The solution (assuming $I(0) = 0$) is:

$$ I(t) = \frac{\mathcal{E}_0}{R} \left[ 1 - e^{-t/\tau} \right] $$

Where $\tau = L/R$ is the time constant. It tells you how long it takes for the current to reach roughly two-thirds of its final value $\mathcal{E}_0/R$.

  • Small $\tau$ (Low $L$ or High $R$): Current jumps to steady-state almost instantly.
  • Large $\tau$ (High $L$ or Low $R$): Current rises slowly, fighting against strong back EMF.
