Deep Dive: Maxwell's Equations in Matter

While Maxwell's equations in their fundamental (vacuum) form are complete, they become incredibly difficult to use inside materials. Why? Because every atom has polarized charges and currents that we don't directly control. To solve this, we reformulate the laws to focus only on the free charges and currents that we actually measure.

1. The Problem of Bound Charges

As we learned in electrostatics and magnetostatics, polarized matter creates its own internal charge and current distributions:

$$ \rho_b = -\div \vec{P} \quad \text{and} \quad \vec{J}_b = \curl \vec{M} $$

In electrodynamics (non-static cases), there is one more crucial effect to consider: the Polarization Current.

The Polarization Current ($\vec{J}_p$)

When electric polarization $\vec{P}$ changes with time, it implies a physical motion of bound charges. If you imagine a small chunk of material where the displacement of charge increases, that motion constitutes a current.

$$ \vec{J}_p = \pd{\vec{P}}{t} $$

Note: This is consistent with the continuity equation, as $\div \vec{J}_p = \pd{}{t}(\div \vec{P}) = -\pd{\rho_b}{t}$.

2. Dividing the Sources

To simplify the equations, we separate the total charge density $\rho$ and current density $\vec{J}$ into free and bound components:

$$ \rho = \rho_f + \rho_b = \rho_f - \div \vec{P} $$ $$ \vec{J} = \vec{J}_f + \vec{J}_b + \vec{J}_p = \vec{J}_f + \curl \vec{M} + \pd{\vec{P}}{t} $$

By substituting these into the fundamental Maxwell's equations, we can "hide" the bound terms inside newly defined auxiliary fields.

3. The Macroscopic Maxwell's Equations

With the auxiliary fields defined as $\vec{D} = \epsilon_0 \vec{E} + \vec{P}$ and $\vec{H} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec{B} - \vec{M}$, the equations take their most convenient form for working with matter:

$$ \begin{align} \text{(i)} \quad & \div \vec{D} = \rho_f \\ \text{(ii)} \quad & \div \vec{B} = 0 \\ \text{(iii)} \quad & \curl \vec{E} = -\pd{\vec{B}}{t} \\ \text{(iv)} \quad & \curl \vec{H} = \vec{J}_f + \pd{\vec{D}}{t} \end{align} $$

This version is called the Macroscopic form. It tells us that:

  • $\vec{D}$ is generated by free charges only.
  • $\vec{H}$ is generated by free currents and changing $\vec{D}$ fields.

4. Constitutive Relations

The equations above are not yet solvable on their own because we have 4 equations but 6 field unknowns ($\vec{E}, \vec{B}, \vec{D}, \vec{H}, \vec{P}, \vec{M}$). We need to know how the material physically responds to the applied fields.

For linear media, these relationships are simple:

$$ \vec{D} = \epsilon \vec{E}, \quad \vec{H} = \frac{1}{\mu} \vec{B} $$

Where $\epsilon = \epsilon_0(1 + \chi_e)$ is the permittivity and $\mu = \mu_0(1 + \chi_m)$ is the permeability of the material.

The Displacement Current Revisited

In this macroscopic context, the quantity $\vec{J}_d = \pd{\vec{D}}{t}$ is the generalized displacement current density. It includes both Maxwell's original vacuum term ($\epsilon_0 \pd{\vec{E}}{t}$) and the physical polarization current ($\pd{\vec{P}}{t}$) resulting from the motion of bound charges.
